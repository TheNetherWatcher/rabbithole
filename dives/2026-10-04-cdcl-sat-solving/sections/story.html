A CDCL solver keeps a trail: the choices it has made, plus everything those choices force. Here are five rules over seven variables. Nothing is decided yet.

Decide, then propagate. The solver guesses x1, then x2. Any clause left with a single open literal forces it. With these two guesses, nothing is forced.

Decide x3. Now C1, C2 and C3 each force one more literal: x4, x5 and x6, all at level 3. Each forced literal records the clause that forced it.

A clash. C4 has all three literals false. Plain search would undo only x3 and retry, and the same clash comes back in another corner. Pre-1996 solvers handled only hundreds of variables this way.

Trace it back. Resolve the clash clause with the clauses that forced its literals, one literal at a time, until a single literal from the current level is left: ¬4. The result is the learned clause (¬2 ∨ ¬4).

Jump back past the innocent choices. The solver returns to level 2 and drops x3. The learned clause now has one open literal, so it forces ¬4 with no search.

The clause stays. It is implied by the formula, so it never changes the answer. It only forbids x2 and x4 being true together, so the solver never walks into that mix again.

Where it still fails. Pigeonhole counting has no short resolution proof, and CDCL works by resolution. For 5 holes CaDiCaL needs 137 conflicts; for 10 holes, 2,657,952.
