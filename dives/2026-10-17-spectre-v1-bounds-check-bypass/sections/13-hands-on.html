spectre_sim.py (in this folder) models the saturating-counter predictor and the 2×+5 vote across rounds of 30 calls, without needing real clflush/rdtscp hardware access.

python3 spectre_sim.py
attack calls/round  success rate   avg rounds   speculation fired
1/30 (train 29)    100.0%           2.1      100.0% of attack calls
5/30 (train 25)    100.0%           1.1      100.0% of attack calls  <- paper's ratio
10/30 (train 20)    100.0%           1.0      100.0% of attack calls
15/30 (train 15)    100.0%           1.0      100.0% of attack calls
25/30 (train 5)    100.0%           3.2        6.7% of attack calls
29/30 (train 1)      0.0%         999.0        0.0% of attack calls
  • At the paper's own ratio (5 attack calls per 30), speculation fires on essentially every attack call, and the vote usually resolves within 1–2 rounds in this model.
  • Push past roughly 20 attack calls per 30 and the saturating counter can't recover toward "in bounds" between attacks — speculation mostly stops firing, and the attack can fail entirely even with all 999 rounds available.
  • This confirms the mechanism's core claim: mistraining isn't about sheer repetition, it's about keeping the predictor's short-memory counter saturated the moment the attack call runs.

For the real hardware experiment (needs a Spectre-vulnerable x86-64 Linux box), compile and run the paper's reference PoC from the official disclosure site, spectreattack.com, and watch readMemoryByte recover bytes of its own hardcoded secret string.
