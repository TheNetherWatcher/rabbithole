Picture the router buffer as a grid. One cell is 4 packets. With no queue, the link carries 12 Mbit/s with a 40 ms round trip. The buffer holds 320 packets, 8 times the bandwidth-delay product.

The old way: keep sending until something drops. Reno and CUBIC grow their rate until the buffer overflows, so the buffer stays full. In the toy sim the median round trip is 221 ms, more than five times the empty 40 ms.

The key idea: measure the pipe. BBR tracks the bottleneck rate (BtlBw) and the shortest round trip (RTprop). Their product is the BDP: 12 Mbit/s × 40 ms = 40 packets, enough to fill the pipe with no queue.

Send about one BDP and stop. BBR paces packets at that size, so the queue stays near empty. In the same sim the median round trip drops to 55.5 ms.

Probe for more. Every so often BBR sends 25% faster to check whether the link got faster. That is the 1.25 pacing gain.

Then ease off. The 0.75 phase drains what the probe added. Over a full cycle the average pacing gain is 1.0, so the queue does not keep growing.

The cost: shallow buffers. With a small buffer, BBRv1 ignores loss and keeps pushing. In the sim it drops 324 packets where Reno drops 8.

The cost: fairness. Against CUBIC, BBR takes a roughly fixed 40% share (Ware et al. 2019). The cards below cover the mechanism and these trade-offs.

The numbers. Same link, same path: 221 ms with the loss-based way, 55.5 ms with BBR, and about 40 packets in flight. Start with the mechanism card below.
