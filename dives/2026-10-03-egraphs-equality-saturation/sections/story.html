Take the term (a×2)÷2. Each subterm becomes an e-class: a box that holds every form known to equal it. The 2 is stored once, even though it appears twice.

The naive way. A compiler applies one rewrite and throws the old form away. Rewrite a×2 to a≪1 first, and the ÷ rule can no longer find a multiply, so a is gone. Fixed pass orders are the usual cure, and they only move the problem.

Keep both. The rewrite adds a≪1 to the same class instead of replacing a×2. Both forms now sit in e2, and neither one is thrown away.

div-assoc rewrites (a×2)÷2 as a×(2÷2) and adds that form to the root class. The new 2÷2 gets its own class, e6. Still nothing is removed.

div-same says x÷x = 1, so e6 now holds 1 as well. Rebuilding then fixes the stored key of every node that points at e6, so the graph stays consistent.

mul-one says a×1 = a. Because e6 holds 1, the root's a×(2÷2) matches, and the root merges into e0. e0 now contains a×1, which points back to e0: a cycle that stands for a, a×1, a×1×1, and so on.

Extraction. Each class keeps its cheapest form. a costs 1 and (a×2)÷2 costs 5, so the answer is a. The multiply and the divide both drop out of the output.

The limit. Add commutativity, a×b = b×a, and the graph keeps growing: 9, 12, 13, 20 and up to 103 e-nodes before the iteration limit stops it. Termination is undecidable in general, so limits are required.
