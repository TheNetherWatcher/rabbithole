Go 1.5 (2015) made marking concurrent with a Dijkstra insertion barrier: on *slot = ptr, shade ptr. That keeps the strong invariant for the heap: no black heap object ever points to a white one.

Stacks are the catch. The compiler emits no barrier for stack writes (a local assignment is the most common operation in a program), so a stack can pick up a white pointer silently. Go 1.7 handled this by treating stacks as permanently grey: scanning a stack colors it black, but the moment its goroutine runs again it can hold a white pointer, so it is grey again. Marking therefore cannot finish until every stack touched since its scan is scanned again, with the world stopped, because a running goroutine could re-dirty it.

So the pause was unbounded in exactly the quantity Go encourages you to make large. Proposal 17503 (Clements and Hudson, 2016-10-21) set out to delete the re-scan. Its preliminary experiments put worst-case STW under 50 µs.

Why not put a barrier on stack writes? Because a barrier is a load, a compare and a possible call. Putting that on every local pointer assignment would tax all code, all the time, to protect a rare race. The design goal was the opposite: keep stack writes free and make the heap barrier smart enough that a scanned stack is provably safe.
