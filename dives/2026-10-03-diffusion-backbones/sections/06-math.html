6.1 The objective, and why the prediction target is a design choice

Forward process: x_t = α_t·x₀ + σ_t·ε, with ε ~ N(0, I).

  • DDPM/VP: α_t = √ᾱ_t, σ_t = √(1−ᾱ_t).
  • Rectified flow (SD3/Flux): α_t = 1−t, σ_t = t.

DDPM's loss (Eq. 14):

L = Et,x₀,ε ‖ ε − εθ(x_t, t, c) ‖²→ in plain terms: the network sees a noisy image and the noise level, and must guess the noise that was added.

Given x_t and t, the three targets are interchangeable:

x̂₀ = (x_t − σ_t ε̂)/α_t, and for rectified flow v = ε − x₀.

So why does the choice matter? Because the network has to output that target with finite capacity.

  • x₀ lies on a low-dimensional image manifold.
  • ε (and v, which contains ε) is full-rank noise in the ambient dimension D.

JiT's argument (Li & He 2025): with 16×16×3 = 768-dimensional pixel patches and a narrower bottleneck, predicting ε or v means carrying 768 dimensions of incompressible noise through the network.

  • ε- and v-prediction fail outright (FID above 96, versus 8.62 for x-prediction in their ablation).
  • x-prediction, converted to a v-loss, works.

The toy experiment behind this: a 2-D spiral buried in a 16-dimensional space, with a 5-layer MLP as the generator. The plots show generated points; the x-prediction samples (left) keep to the spiral, while the ε-prediction samples (right) scatter off it.

In plain terms: asking a network to output something that lives on a small manifold is easier than asking it to reproduce random noise. This is invisible in 4-channel latents (D is small) and decisive in pixel space or high-dimensional latents.

6.2 adaLN-Zero: how t gets into every layer

With c = emb(t) + emb(y), one linear layer produces six vectors per block (DiT code):

(β₁, γ₁, α₁, β₂, γ₂, α₂) = W·SiLU(c) + b, with W = 0 and b = 0 at init
x ← x + α₁ ⊙ Attn( (1+γ₁) ⊙ LN(x) + β₁ )
x ← x + α₂ ⊙ MLP ( (1+γ₂) ⊙ LN(x) + β₂ )→ in plain terms: the timestep rescales and shifts every normalized activation, and a gate α decides how much each block contributes.

Why this form:

  • (1+γ), not γ. With γ=0 at init the scale is the identity, not zero.
  • The gate α, zero-initialised. Every block starts as an identity residual, so the 28-layer network starts as "output = input projection". Gradients flow cleanly, which is the same trick as Fixup / zero-init ResNets.
  • Why not just add t as a token? In-context conditioning has to fight for attention mass and influence the network through one token. Modulation touches every channel of every token directly.

6.3 Cost: why tokens, not parameters, are the bill

For T tokens of width d with a 4× MLP, forward multiply-accumulates (MACs) per layer are:

C(T, d) = 4Td² (QKV + output) + 8Td² (MLP) + 2T²d (QKᵀ and AV) = 12Td² + 2T²d→ in plain terms: the linear layers cost scales with tokens × width², and attention costs scales with tokens² × width.

The ratio is attention/linear = T/(6d), so attention dominates once T > 6d. For Flux (d=3072) that means beyond about 18K tokens. Images at 1024² sit below the crossover: 4,608 tokens means about 20% of layer MACs. Video at 720p×129 frames (about 119K tokens) is about 87% attention.

Check against the DiT paper (run backbone_flops.py, in this folder, as in §13): DiT-XL/2 gives N=28, T=256, d=1152, so 28·C = 118.4 G vs 118.64 reported. Every DiT config matches within 0.2–5%. DiT's 512² model (T=1024) is reported at 524.6, and the formula gives 524.3. So DiT's "Gflops" are effectively forward GMACs (my inference; the paper does not say so).

Patch size enters as T = (I/p)². Halving p quadruples T and at least quadruples cost, while parameters stay constant. This is why DiT found that Gflops, not parameters, predict FID: S/2 and B/4 have similar Gflops and similar FID (DiT Fig. 8).

Cost calculator: tokens are the bill.

Per layer MACs = 12·T·d² + 2·T²·d. Move the sliders and watch the attention share cross 50% once T > 6d.

6.4 Resolution-dependent timestep shift

At higher resolution, neighbouring pixels are redundant, so the same σ destroys less information (simple diffusion). SD3 maps a timestep t_n tuned at n pixels to m pixels (SD3 §5.3.2):

t_m = √(m/n)·t_n / (1 + (√(m/n) − 1)·t_n)→ in plain terms: bigger images spend more of the schedule at high noise. For example, with √(m/n)=3, t_n = 0.5 maps to t_m = 0.75.

SD3 used a shift of 3.0 at 1024². RAE uses the same form with m = effective latent dimension, not pixel count. That shift alone moved its FID from 23.1 to 4.81 (RAE). The backbone takes the blame when this is wrong, but the schedule is the cause.

6.5 RAE's width condition

RAE (Theorem 1, arXiv 2510.11690): once Gaussian noise is added, the support of x_t is all of ℝD, even if the data sits on a low-dimensional manifold. A denoiser whose per-token width d < D (the token dimension) therefore cannot represent the optimal denoiser, and it incurs an irreducible loss.

In plain terms: your transformer must be at least as wide as the tokens it is denoising. In their experiments DiT-S (width 384) fails on DINOv2's 768-dimensional tokens, and it works once width ≥ 768. This is a backbone-sizing rule that comes from the latent space.
