6.1 Amplification from retries

Let each attempt fail (time out) independently with probability p, and let the client make up to r retries. Expected attempts per user request:

E[A] = Σi=0r pi = (1 − pr+1) / (1 − p)→ in plain terms: while things are healthy (p ≈ 0) each request costs ~1 attempt. As failure approaches certainty (p → 1) each request costs r+1 attempts.

Offered load at the server:

λeff(p) = λ · (1 − pr+1) / (1 − p),   λeff(0) = λ,   λeff(1) = (r+1)λ

With d layers each retrying independently, the factors multiply: (r+1)d. Google's SRE book: 3 layers × 4 attempts = 4³ = 64× on the database. AWS's Builders' Library cites 243× for a five-deep stack, which is 3⁵ (three attempts per layer). Some summaries describe that as "three retries per layer", but 3 retries would give 4⁵ = 1,024. (SRE)

6.2 Two fixed points (bistability)

The failure probability is itself a function of load. With FIFO and timeout T, an attempt fails if its queueing delay exceeds T. When λeff > μ the queue grows without bound, so delay → ∞ and p → 1. When λeff < μ the queue is short, so delay ≪ T and p ≈ 0. Look for self-consistent states p* = f(λeff(p*)):

  • Healthy: p ≈ 0 ⇒ λeff ≈ λ. Self-consistent iff λ < μ.
  • Collapsed: p ≈ 1 ⇒ λeff ≈ (r+1)λ. Self-consistent iff (r+1)λ > μ.

Both hold when

μ / (r+1) < λ < μ→ in plain terms: in this band the system has two stable states, and nothing in the current load tells you which one you're in. Only history (whether something kicked you) decides. Below μ/(r+1) the collapsed state can't sustain itself. With μ=100 and r=2 the band is 33–100 req/s, exactly where the sim flips. (This fixed-point framing is my synthesis. HotOS'21's Fig. 2 shows the same band, and Brooker called it "bistability" in 2019.)

Farahbakhsh et al. draw the same two-tendency picture as a vector field: one tendency points toward zero, the other runs off toward saturation.

Why this form and not a simpler one: a single-equilibrium model ("load > capacity ⇒ slow") can't explain why cutting load from 70 back to 70 doesn't fix anything. You need a load-dependent amplification (p depends on load, and load depends on p). That loop is the sustaining effect in algebra.

6.3 OSDI'22's general version

Huang et al. measure load L and capacity C in abstract resource units (RU/s). "Organic" quantities (Lorg, Corg) include the trigger but not the amplification; "system" quantities (Lsys, Csys) include both. They define two amplification factors:

αL(t) = Lsys(t) / Lorg(t) ≤ w*L,    αC(t) = Csys(t) / Corg(t) ≥ 1 / w*C
  • w*L is the worst-case workload amplification: retries, or more expensive requests such as error logging.
  • w*C is the worst-case capacity degradation: GC, or a cache whose loss pushes the hit traffic onto the DB.

Theorem 2 (stable region).

Cstable = Cnorm / (w*L · w*C)→ in plain terms: your real safe capacity is your advertised capacity divided by the worst amplification your own policies can produce. If normal load Lnorm < Cstable, no trigger can cause a metastable failure. Check it against the examples: 1 retry → w*L=2 → 300/2 = 150 QPS (HotOS DB example). Cache with 90% hit rate → w*C = 10 → 3000/10 = 300 QPS hidden capacity. "A policy with at most two retries will not amplify the work more than three times, while the policy with no cap effectively leaves the system with no stable region" (OSDI'22).

Theorem 1 (necessary trigger): a trigger can only matter if mtrigL + mtrigC ≥ Cnorm − Lnorm, i.e. the load spike plus the capacity drop must eat the whole headroom.

Theorem 3 (degrees of vulnerability): if wL(Δt) · wC(Δt) < Cnorm/Lnorm, a trigger lasting Δt can't tip the system. The amplification functions grow with trigger duration (retries only start after the timeout), so vulnerability is a region in (load × trigger size × trigger duration), not a yes/no.

→ in plain terms: this is why 78% CPU loss for 10 s recovered and 80% for 10 s didn't in their MongoDB reproduction, and why 80% for 9 s also recovered: "a 2%-decrease in available CPU or a 1-second increase in duration separated successful recovery from a metastable failure."

6.4 How long recovery takes

Once the loop is broken, a backlog B drains at the spare rate:

tdrain = B / (μ − λeff)→ in plain terms: near the edge of the band the denominator goes to zero and recovery time blows up. That is the sim's λ=30 run (μ − 3λ = 10/s, ~80 s). Amazon's "insurmountable backlog" article gives the operational version: a one-hour outage needs double capacity for another hour to drain. Dropping expired work changes the numerator from "all queued work" to "only live work", which is why deadline-aware dequeue recovers in ~0 s.

6.5 The 2025 formalisation: metastability as a CTMC property

Alvaro, Isaacs, Majumdar et al. (arXiv 2510.03551) model a server as a continuous-time Markov chain over state (q, o) = (queue length, "orbit" = requests waiting to retry). Their definition: a CTMC is metastable if it has ≥ 2 disjoint sets of states such that the expected time to move between sets is much larger than the time to mix within a set. Spectrally, the generator matrix has a large gap after its first few eigenvalues, so the slow modes are the "valleys". In their toy model (1 thread at 10 RPS, queue bound 150, 5 RPS offered, 5 s timeout, 5 retries) the vector field shows a tipping region around q ≈ 40 with ≥ 30 in orbit.
