MoE routing and load balancing: from aux-loss to aux-loss-free

A Mixture-of-Experts router sends each token to its top-k experts, and left alone it collapses onto a few favourites, so the busiest expert sets the speed of the whole layer. The classic fix adds a loss that fights the real objective. DeepSeek-V3 instead keeps a per-expert bias that steers selection only, not the gate, and a toy controller with u = 0.001 settles where no balancing stays at MaxVio 2.57.

Why it matters: under expert parallelism every layer waits for its hottest expert, and at 3B the aux-loss method sits at MaxVio 0.52 against 0.04 for the bias method.

Plain English
In everyday words

A nurse with sticky notes, not fines

A triage nurse sends each word to 8 of 256 specialist doctors. The busiest doctors get more traffic, so the nurse keeps favouring them. The fix leaves the nurse alone and puts a sticky note on each door, nudging close calls toward idle doctors.

Picture a hospital with 256 specialist doctors and a triage nurse at the door. Each patient (a word, or token, in a sentence) sees the nurse for a second, and the nurse sends them to the 8 doctors who look most relevant. Only those 8 do any work, so the hospital can employ a huge staff without every patient costing 256 consultations. That is a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language model: huge total knowledge, small cost per word.

The catch: the nurse learns from outcomes. Doctors who see more patients get better at their job, so the nurse sends them even more patients, and the rest sit idle and never improve. Soon 5 doctors do everything and 250 are wasted. Worse, on a real hospital floor the busiest doctor’s queue sets how long everyone waits. Keeping the queues even is load balancing.

The classic fix is to punish the nurse with an extra fairness penalty on top of the real goal. It works, but the penalty fights the real goal: the nurse stops sending patients to the best doctor just to look fair. The newer fix (DeepSeek, 2024) leaves the nurse alone and puts a sticky note on each doctor’s door: “busy, −0.001” or “idle, +0.001”. The nurse still decides by merit, but the notes tilt close calls toward idle doctors. Notes only change who gets picked, never how much that doctor’s opinion counts.

Everyday example

1,000 patients arrive, each needs 2 doctors, so 2,000 consultations. With 4 doctors the fair share is 500 each. Doctor A gets 820, B 430, C 300, D 450. After the shift, A’s note goes down a notch and B, C and D go up a notch. Next shift, a patient for whom A and B score nearly the same goes to B. Repeat for 400 shifts and everyone sits near 500. Nobody was fined.

Context
§1 · Prerequisites

Six prerequisites: router, top-k, all-to-all, capacity

  • Router: a linear map from a token to N expert logits.
  • Top-k: only k experts run per token.
  • Expert parallelism: experts sharded across GPUs, tokens moved by all-to-all.
  • Capacity factor: a hard per-expert cap, with overflow dropped.
  • Transformer block = attention + a position-wise feed-forward network (FFN). MoE replaces the one FFN with N parallel FFNs (“experts”) plus a router. Shazeer 2017
  • Router = a linear map W_g ∈ R^{d×N} from a token’s hidden state u to N logits. Top-k picks experts; the output is the gate-weighted sum of their outputs.
  • Total params ≠ active params. FLOPs per token scale with the k active experts; memory scales with all N experts.
  • Expert parallelism (EP): experts are sharded across GPUs. Tokens move with all-to-all dispatch and combine, so the slowest GPU gates each layer.
  • Top-k is non-differentiable with respect to which experts win. The gradient reaches the router only through the gate values of the chosen experts.
  • Capacity factor (CF): a hard per-expert token cap of CF · T/N. Overflow tokens are dropped and skip the layer via the residual. Switch Transformer
§1b · Real-world context

Where MoE ships, and the knobs you actually set

Mixtral, DeepSeek-V3, OLMoE, Qwen MoE and DBRX all route tokens to top-k experts. You set num_experts_per_tok, the balance-loss coefficient, the capacity factor and the EP size. Prefer dense for single-GPU or low-traffic serving.

What it is. A sparsely activated FFN: N experts, and a per-token router picks k of them. Compute per token is roughly a dense model with k experts; capacity is roughly N experts.

Why it exists. Dense scaling cost grows with every parameter. Sparse gating decouples capacity from FLOPs. Shazeer 2017 reached 137B parameters with up to 4096 experts; GShard (2020) reached 600B+ on 2048 TPUv3 in 4 days. Without balancing, learned routers collapse onto a few experts (see §9), which wastes the extra parameters and breaks EP efficiency.

How it’s used. The practitioner touches are num_experts, num_experts_per_tok (top-k), the balance-loss coefficient, the capacity factor and the EP size.

# Megatron-Core style flags (values as reported by implementation research; check your version's docs)
--num-experts 8 --moe-router-topk 2 \
--moe-router-load-balancing-type aux_loss --moe-aux-loss-coeff 1e-2 \
--moe-grouped-gemm --expert-model-parallel-size 4
# the router, minimal (token-choice, Mixtral-like)
logits = h @ W_gate                          # [T, N]
w, idx = torch.topk(logits, k, dim=-1)       # select
w = torch.softmax(w, dim=-1)                 # gate over the k winners only
out = sum(w[:, j:j+1] * experts[idx[:, j]](h) ...)   # grouped GEMM in practice

When to use it, and when not. Use MoE for high-concurrency serving and training, where FLOPs are the bill and VRAM and interconnect are available. Prefer dense models for low-traffic or single-GPU deployments (all N experts must still sit in memory: a 400B-total MoE needs multiple GPUs just to load), for strict latency-variance needs, or when all-to-all bandwidth is lacking. Signs of a wrong choice: GPU utilisation under about 50% at inference, a wide gap between p50 and p99 step time, or one hot-expert GPU pegged while the others idle.

Who should care. ML engineers pretraining MoEs (the balance method decides quality), infra and serving engineers (stragglers, batch-dependent routing), and security engineers (cross-batch leaks, §9).

Technical deep dive
§2 · The one-liner

“Top-k plus a balance loss” hides five things

Balance has three scopes. The aux loss is a second objective on the same weights. Selection and weighting can use different scores. Collapse is a feedback loop. And at inference, your batch-mates decide your routing.

“A router sends each token to its top-k experts, and a balancing loss keeps experts evenly used.” It hides:

  1. Balance has two meanings: per sequence, per batch, or per corpus. They are different constraints with different costs to quality.
  2. The aux loss is a second objective whose gradient lands on the same router weights as the LM loss.
  3. Selection and weighting need not use the same score. That separation is the whole trick of loss-free balancing.
  4. Routing collapse is a feedback loop, not a bug in initialisation.
  5. At inference, nobody balances for you: routing depends on who shares your batch.
§3 · The problem

The busiest expert sets the speed of every layer

Under expert parallelism the max expert load gates each layer, not the mean. In a toy run with mean 500 and max 1,750, the layer takes about 3.5× the balanced time. Capacity caps then turn the skew into dropped tokens.

  • Compute per layer is gated by the max expert load under EP, not the mean. With a mean of 500 and a max of 1,750 (my toy run below: MaxVio ≈ 2.5), the layer takes about 3.5× the balanced time.
  • Capacity caps turn imbalance into waste. Switch uses CF 1.0–2.0; ST-MoE uses 1.25 in training and 2.0 in evaluation. Overflow becomes dropped tokens (a quality loss) or padding (wasted FLOPs).
  • Unbalanced training starves experts of gradient, so the extra parameters are dead weight.
§4 · Mental model

A triage nurse, and where the analogy breaks

The nurse-and-doctors picture carries the feedback loop. It breaks because tokens are not interchangeable: similar tokens going to similar experts is the point of specialization, and it is also what skews the load when the data is skewed.

A triage nurse with doctors (§0b). Where it breaks: unlike patients, tokens are not interchangeable. “Similar tokens go to similar experts” is desired (that is specialization), but it is exactly what makes the load skewed when the data is skewed. Perfect balance and perfect specialization conflict, and the field disagrees about where to set the dial (§11b).

§5 · Mechanism, traced

One token through four experts, then one bias update

Sigmoid affinities plus a running bias pick experts 3 and 1 instead of 0 and 3. The gate still uses the unbiased scores. After the batch, loads of [820, 430, 300, 450] move the bias by 0.001 per step, with no gradient involved.

Step-through of the routing inside.

DeepSeek-V3-style routing (sigmoid affinities), with 4 experts and k = 2 for readability. One token has router logits u·e_i = [1.2, 0.4, −0.3, 0.9]. V3 uses sigmoid, not softmax.

affinity s_i = sigmoid(logit)    = [0.769, 0.599, 0.426, 0.711]
bias b_i (running state)         = [-0.20, +0.05, +0.12,  0.00]   # expert 0 has been hot
selection score s_i + b_i        = [0.569, 0.649, 0.546, 0.711]
top-2 of selection score         -> experts {3, 1}      (unbiased top-2 would be {0, 3})
gate g'_i = s_i for chosen       = {e1: 0.599, e3: 0.711}   # UNBIASED
g_i = g'_i / sum(g')             = {e1: 0.457, e3: 0.543}
y = x + 0.457*E1(x) + 0.543*E3(x) (+ shared expert output)

After the batch (T = 1000 tokens, k = 2, so 2000 assignments and a mean load of 500), the loads are [820, 430, 300, 450]:

e_i = mean - load_i = [-320, +70, +200, +50]
b_i += gamma * sign(e_i), gamma = 0.001   ->  b = [-0.201, +0.051, +0.121, +0.001]

No gradient touched anything. The bias is controller state, not a parameter. Selection is not weighting: expert 0 lost the slot, but its gate math is unchanged for tokens that still pick it.

§6 · The math

The aux loss is a second objective; the bias is a controller

The Switch aux loss is α N Σ f_i P_i, and its gradient fights the LM loss on the same router weights. Loss-free balancing is an integral controller on load, b_i += u·sign(mean − load_i), with u = 0.001 and no gradient path.

Switch/GShard aux loss

L_aux = α · N · Σ_i f_i · P_i, with f_i = (1/T) Σ_x 1[argmax p(x) = i] (hard fraction) and P_i = (1/T) Σ_x p_i(x) (soft probability mass). N = experts, T = tokens, α = 1e-2 in Switch. → in plain terms: how much of the traffic goes to expert i, times how much the router is leaning toward it, summed over experts.
  • Under uniform routing, f_i = P_i = 1/N, so L = αN · N · (1/N²) = α. By a Cauchy–Schwarz-type rearrangement, this is the minimum for a fixed Σ P_i = 1.
  • Why a product, not f² or P²? f_i is non-differentiable (argmax). Using f_i · P_i lets the gradient flow through P_i, weighted by how loaded expert i actually is: ∂L/∂p_i(x) = α N f_i / T. Popular experts get pushed down in proportion, and the loss stays differentiable.

→ in plain terms: the penalty pushes the router’s probability down on experts in proportion to how overloaded they are.

Router z-loss (ST-MoE)

L_z = (1/B) Σ_x (log Σ_j e^{x_j})², c_z = 1e-3. → in plain terms: penalises large router logits, to keep the softmax numerically sane.

Loss-free balancing (Wang et al. 2024; DeepSeek-V3)

Selection: s_i + b_i ∈ TopK(·). Gate: from the unbiased s_i. Update: b_i ← b_i + u · sign(c̄ − c_i), where c_i is the number of tokens routed to expert i in the batch and c̄ is the mean. → in plain terms: an integral-style controller on load.

Reported u = 0.001 (Wang) and γ = 0.001 for the first 14.3T tokens, then 0.0 for the last 500B (V3).

Why sign and not a proportional update? The step size is independent of how large the violation is, so one wildly hot expert does not make the bias overshoot. The cost is a limit cycle with amplitude about u in score space. In the demo below, a u that is too big gives permanent jitter.

Why there is no gradient interference. ∂L_LM/∂W sees only the LM objective, and b has no dependence on W through autograd. The aux-loss approach adds α N f_i ∂P_i/∂W to the same W: if α is too small, the router collapses; if α is too large, the router logits are pulled toward uniform regardless of input, erasing specialization.

V3’s complement. A sequence-wise balance loss with α = 0.0001 remains, “just to avoid extreme imbalance within any single sequence”.

§7 · Why it works

Selection and weighting can use different scores

Balance is a constraint on how often experts are selected, and an additive offset can steer selection without changing the scoring function. Gate from the raw score, or the bias leaks into the output. Remove the bias and MaxVio stays near 2.57.

The core insight: balance is a constraint on selection frequency, and selection frequency can be steered by an additive offset without altering the learned scoring function. Ablations:

  • Remove the bias and keep nothing: skewed expert popularity persists. In my demo, MaxVio stays at about 2.57 with u = 0.
  • Use the biased score for gating too: the bias leaks into the output weights, and the bias value becomes part of the function the model must learn around. This is why V3 takes the gate from the raw score.
  • Remove the node limit (V3’s M = 4): balance is fine, but cross-node all-to-all grows.
  • Keep an aux loss with a big α: balance improves, but perplexity gets worse. Wang reports Loss-Controlled MaxVio of 0.72 and 0.52 versus 0.04 for Loss-Free, at 1B and 3B.
§8 · Design knobs

Granularity, shared experts and balance scope are the dials

More, smaller experts give more combinations and better specialization, but more all-to-all. Shared experts absorb common knowledge. Balance scope runs from sequence to micro-batch to global batch. Expert Choice gives perfect balance but breaks batch independence.

§9 · Where it breaks

Collapse loops, micro-batch bugs and batch-coupled leaks

Collapse is self-reinforcing. Micro-batch balance loss punishes code-only sequences for using code experts. Under capacity limits, an attacker’s co-batched queries can drop your tokens: in Hayes et al., a victim’s tokens fall from 6 to 0 in one expert.

Two figures from the attack paper inside.

  • Collapse loop: a popular expert gets more gradient, which makes it better, which makes it more popular.
  • Aux-loss dilemma (§6): too small and the router collapses, too large and specialization is erased.
  • Micro-batch balancing bug (Qiu et al. 2025): frameworks compute the load-balance loss per micro-batch of 1–4 sequences, which forces within-sequence uniformity. That punishes a code-only sequence for using the code experts. Global-batch balance loss fixes it.
  • Stragglers: EP all-to-all is a synchronous barrier, so one hot GPU stalls all of them.
  • Inference: there is no controller at serve time, so routing depends on batch composition.
  • Security: cross-batch dependencies leak. Hayes, Shumailov and Yona (2024, Buffer Overflow in Mixture of Experts) show that token dropping under capacity limits lets an attacker’s co-batched queries alter a victim’s output. Figure 1 shows the flow. Figure 3 shows the attack in numbers: the victim’s tokens fall from 6 to 0 as adversarial tokens fill the expert’s 20-slot buffer.

Yona, Shumailov, Hayes and Carlini (2024, Stealing User Prompts) exploit Expert-Choice routing plus CUDA topk tie-breaking to recover a victim prompt with about 100 queries per token, shown on a two-layer Mixtral.

  • Misconception: “experts specialise in domains”. Wang, Hayou and Nalisnick (2026) argue that routing reflects the geometry of the hidden space. Mixtral’s own analysis found syntactic, token-level rather than topical specialization.
  • Unverified in my research (excluded from the dive): a quoted 85% speedup from dropping 12% of tokens; a 79.2% all-to-all latency share; specific validation-loss numbers for sequence- versus batch-wise losses from secondary summaries.
§10 · In the wild

DeepSeek-V3, Mixtral, Megablocks and OLMoE choose differently

V3 uses a sigmoid score, a bias with γ = 0.001 that is later annealed to 0, and a tiny sequence-wise loss. Mixtral and Megatron use a Switch-style aux loss near 1e-2. Megablocks makes imbalance free in FLOPs, and OLMoE is dropless.

  • DeepSeek-V3: bias γ = 0.001, then 0.0; sigmoid with normalisation; node-limited to M = 4; a tiny sequence-wise loss (α = 1e-4) as a safety net.
  • Mixtral and the Megatron default: Switch-style aux loss with a coefficient of about 1e-2, and a softmax over the top-k logits.
  • Megablocks: reformulates the experts as one block-sparse matmul, so imbalance costs no extra FLOPs. No drops.
  • vLLM and SGLang serving: moe_align_block_size sorts token-expert pairs and pads them to block multiples before the grouped GEMM.
  • OLMoE: dropless token-choice routing. It reports early routing saturation and low expert co-activation.

§11b · Open problems

Balance versus specialization has no agreed optimum

Nobody agrees where to set the balance dial. Decoupling routing from batch composition at inference is unsolved, and it is the same problem as the security leak. Learnability of non-differentiable routers has no sample-complexity theory.

  • The balance versus specialization Pareto frontier has no agreed shape. Batch-wise loosening helps (Qiu), and loss-free matches or beats aux loss (Wang), yet strict balance at inference is still wanted for EP.
  • Training and serving mismatch: the bias controller exists only in training. At serve time, batch makeup decides the load. Decoupling routing from batch composition is unsolved, and it is also the security issue.
  • Learnability of routers: linear routers with top-k have non-differentiable selection. ReLU, Dirichlet and OT routing trade stability for gradient. There is no sample-complexity theory.
  • Optimal granularity beyond small scale.
  • Why the obvious fixes fail: a bigger α kills specialization; per-sequence balance forces uniformity where the data isn’t uniform; expert choice gives balance but breaks batch independence.
§12 · Lineage

From 1991 mixtures to bias-based balancing in 2024

Jacobs 1991 gave mixtures of experts. Shazeer 2017 added noisy top-k and load losses. GShard, Switch and ST-MoE added capacity, the Switch aux loss and the z-loss. DeepSeek-V3 in 2024 swapped the loss for a bias.

Jacobs et al. 1991, mixtures of experts → Shazeer 2017, noisy top-k with importance and load CV² losses → GShard 2020, top-2 with capacity and a random second expert → Switch 2021, top-1 with α N Σ f P → ST-MoE 2022, router z-loss → Expert Choice 2022 → DeepSeekMoE 2024, fine-grained plus shared experts → Loss-Free Balancing and DeepSeek-V3, 2024 → global-batch LBL and differentiable routers, 2025–26.

§14 · Question ladder

Test yourself

20 questions across mechanism, why, counterfactual, adversarial, connection and open. Reveal answers one at a time.

Answer in your head first, then reveal and mark yourself honestly. Marks are saved in this browser and feed the progress ring.

Mechanism
1. Why does top-k plus softmax-over-k give the router gradient only through the chosen experts?

Unselected experts get gate 0 after masking, so their logits do not affect the output. Selection itself is discrete.

→ related card
2. In V3, which score multiplies the expert output, biased or raw?

The raw sigmoid affinity, normalised over the selected set. The bias only affects selection.

→ related card
3. What do f_i and P_i each represent, and why is only P_i differentiable?

f_i is the hard fraction of tokens whose argmax is i (non-differentiable). P_i is the mean router probability (differentiable). The gradient is α N f_i on the P_i path.

→ related card
4. What does capacity CF · T/N do at overflow?

Excess tokens skip the expert via the residual, so they are dropped.

→ related card
5. What is MaxVio?

(max load − mean load) / mean load. 0 means perfect balance.

→ related card
Why
6. Why use sign in the bias update?

It gives a bounded step with no overshoot from outlier loads, and it is simple. The cost is jitter of order u.

→ related card
7. Why anneal γ to 0 at the end of V3 training?

This rationale is my inference. The paper reports the schedule, and the likely purpose is to freeze the biases so the controller’s jitter does not perturb late training.

→ related card
8. Why does a large aux-loss α hurt quality?

Its gradient pulls router logits toward uniform regardless of token content, fighting the LM gradient.

→ related card
9. Why does micro-batch balancing hurt specialization?

It forces each tiny batch of 1–4 sequences to use all experts, even when every token is code.

→ related card
10. Why do shared experts help?

Common knowledge lives in the always-on experts, so the routed experts need not duplicate it.

→ related card
Counterfactual
11. If gating used s_i + b_i, what changes?

The bias enters the output, so a controller meant to be neutral perturbs predictions and couples to the gradients.

→ related card
12. If load were measured per sequence instead of per batch?

The constraint is stricter and close to the micro-batch problem. V3 keeps only a tiny α for that purpose.

→ related card
13. What if N doubles at fixed active FLOPs?

More combinations and usually better quality (granularity scaling), but more all-to-all traffic and memory. Balance is harder per expert, though each expert matters less.

→ related card
Adversarial
14. How can an attacker in your batch change your output when capacity limits exist?

By flooding an expert so your tokens overflow and are dropped or rerouted (Hayes et al. 2024).

→ related card
15. Why does expert choice leak?

Experts choose across the whole batch, which couples the tokens of different users. CUDA topk tie-breaking makes the coupling exploitable (Yona et al. 2024).

→ related card
16. How could you game a bias controller?

Speculatively, by skewing traffic so the biases drift. It matters only if the bias is shared across users’ batches in serving. This is not shown in the literature I verified.

→ related card
Connection
17. How is the bias controller like a PI loop or TCP congestion control?

Both use feedback on an error signal, and the step size trades speed against oscillation.
