MoE routing and load balancing: from aux-loss to aux-loss-free

TL;DR. A Mixture-of-Experts layer sends each token to k of N expert FFNs. Left alone, the router collapses onto a few experts. The classic cure adds a balancing loss that fights the language-model loss. DeepSeek's cure (Wang et al. 2024, used in DeepSeek-V3) adds a per-expert bias that only affects which experts are selected, never how much their output counts. No extra gradient, much better balance.

Plain English first

Explain it simply. Picture a hospital with 256 specialist doctors and a triage nurse at the door. Each patient (a word in a sentence) sees the nurse for a second, who sends them to the 8 most relevant doctors. Only those 8 work, so the hospital can employ a huge staff without every patient costing 256 consultations. That is a Mixture-of-Experts language model: huge total knowledge, small cost per word.

The catch: the nurse learns from outcomes. Doctors who see more patients get better, so the nurse sends them even more, and the rest never improve. Soon 5 doctors do everything. And the busiest doctor's queue sets how long everyone waits. Keeping queues even is load balancing.

The classic fix punishes the nurse with a fairness penalty, which fights the real goal: the nurse stops picking the best doctor just to look fair. The newer fix puts a sticky note on each doctor's door: "busy, -0.001" or "idle, +0.001". The nurse still decides by merit; the notes tilt close calls toward idle doctors, and never change how much a doctor's opinion counts.

Everyday example. 1,000 patients, each needs 2 doctors: 2,000 consultations, fair share 500 each among 4 doctors. A gets 820, B 430, C 300, D 450. After the shift A's note goes down a notch, B/C/D up a notch. Repeat for 400 shifts and everyone sits near 500. Nobody was fined.

Now the same thing, technically.

Real-world context

What it is. A sparsely-activated FFN: N experts, a per-token router picks k. Compute per token ≈ k experts; capacity ≈ N experts.

Why it exists. Dense scaling cost grows with every parameter. Sparse gating decouples capacity from FLOPs. Shazeer 2017 reached 137B params with up to 4096 experts; GShard 2020 hit 600B+ on 2048 TPUv3 in 4 days. Without balancing, learned routers collapse and waste the extra parameters.

How it's used

# Megatron-Core style flags (reported values; check your version's docs)
--num-experts 8 --moe-router-topk 2 \
--moe-router-load-balancing-type aux_loss --moe-aux-loss-coeff 1e-2 \
--moe-grouped-gemm --expert-model-parallel-size 4

# the router, minimal (token-choice, Mixtral-like)
logits = h @ W_gate                          # [T, N]
w, idx = torch.topk(logits, k, dim=-1)       # select
w = torch.softmax(w, dim=-1)                 # gate over k winners only
out = sum(w[:, j:j+1] * experts[idx[:, j]](h) ...)   # grouped GEMM in practice

Use MoE when

  • High-concurrency serving or big training budgets: FLOPs are the bill
  • You have VRAM and fast interconnect for all N experts

Skip when

  • Low traffic or single-GPU (all experts must still fit in memory)
  • Strict latency variance, weak all-to-all bandwidth
  • Signs you chose wrong: GPU util under ~50%, wide p50/p99 gap, one GPU pegged

Who should care. ML engineers pretraining MoEs; infra/serving engineers (stragglers, batch-dependent routing); security engineers (cross-batch leaks).

Technical deep dive

Bare-minimum context

  • Transformer block = attention + FFN; MoE swaps the FFN for N experts plus a router (Shazeer 2017).
  • Router = linear map from token state to N logits; top-k pick experts; output is the gate-weighted sum.
  • Total params ≠ active params: FLOPs scale with k, memory with N.
  • Expert parallelism (EP): experts sharded across GPUs; all-to-all dispatch/combine; the slowest GPU gates each layer.
  • Top-k selection is non-differentiable; gradient reaches the router only via chosen experts' gate values.
  • Capacity factor CF: per-expert cap CF·T/N; overflow tokens are dropped via residual (Switch).

The one-liner and what it hides

"A router sends each token to its top-k experts, and a balancing loss keeps experts evenly used." It hides:

  1. Balance has several scopes (sequence, batch, corpus) with different quality costs.
  2. The aux loss is a second objective whose gradient lands on the same router weights as the LM loss.
  3. Selection and weighting need not use the same score. That is the trick of loss-free balancing.
  4. Collapse is a feedback loop, not an init bug.
  5. At inference nobody balances for you: routing depends on who shares your batch.

The problem

  • Layer time is gated by the max expert load under EP. My toy run below: mean 500, max ≈ 3.5× mean (MaxVio ≈ 2.5) → ~3.5× the balanced time.
  • Capacity caps (Switch CF 1.0–2.0; ST-MoE 1.25 train / 2.0 eval) turn imbalance into dropped tokens or padding FLOPs.
  • Starved experts get little gradient, so extra parameters are dead weight.

Mental model

Triage nurse plus doctors. Where it breaks: tokens aren't interchangeable. "Similar tokens go to similar experts" is what specialization is, and also what makes load skewed when data is skewed. Perfect balance and perfect specialization conflict.

The mechanism, traced

DeepSeek-V3-style (sigmoid affinities), 4 experts, k=2. Step through one token, then one batch update.

affinity s_i = sigmoid(logit) = [0.769, 0.599, 0.426, 0.711]
bias b_i (running state)  = [-0.20, +0.05, +0.12, 0.00]   # expert 0 has been hot
selection score s_i + b_i = [0.569, 0.649, 0.546, 0.711]
top-2 of selection score  -> experts {3, 1}   (unbiased top-2 would be {0, 3})
gates from UNBIASED s     = {e1: 0.599, e3: 0.711} -> normalised {0.457, 0.543}
y = x + 0.457*E1(x) + 0.543*E3(x) (+ shared expert)

batch loads [820,430,300,450], mean 500
e_i = mean - load_i = [-320,+70,+200,+50]
b_i += 0.001 * sign(e_i)  -> [-0.201,+0.051,+0.121,+0.001]

No gradient touched anything: the bias is controller state, not a parameter.

The math

Switch/GShard aux loss

L_aux = α · N · Σ_i f_i · P_i, with f_i = (1/T) Σ_x 1[argmax p(x)=i] (hard fraction), P_i = (1/T) Σ_x p_i(x) (soft mass). N experts, T tokens, α=1e-2 in Switch.

Uniform routing gives f_i=P_i=1/N so L=αN·N·(1/N²)=α, the minimum for fixed ΣP=1. Why a product? f_i is non-differentiable (argmax); the product lets gradient flow through P_i weighted by true load: ∂L/∂p_i(x)=αN·f_i/T.

→ in plain terms: the penalty pushes router probability down on experts in proportion to how overloaded they are.

Router z-loss (ST-MoE)

L_z = (1/B) Σ_x (log Σ_j e^{x_j})², c_z = 1e-3

→ in plain terms: keep router logits small so softmax stays numerically sane.

Loss-free balancing (Wang et al. 2024; DeepSeek-V3)

selection: s_i + b_i ∈ TopK(·) · gate from unbiased s_i · update b_i ← b_i + u·sign(c̄ − c_i)

c_i = tokens routed to expert i this batch; c̄ = mean. Wang: u=0.001. V3: γ=0.001 for the first 14.3T tokens, then 0.0 for the last 500B.

→ in plain terms: an integral-style controller on load. sign not proportional: step size is independent of violation size, so one wild expert doesn't make the bias overshoot. Cost: a limit cycle of amplitude ≈ u (see widget).

Why no interference. b has no autograd path into W. The aux loss instead adds αN f_i ∂P_i/∂W to the same weights the LM loss trains: α small → collapse; α large → logits pulled to uniform regardless of input. V3 keeps a sequence-wise loss with α=0.0001 "just to avoid extreme imbalance within any single sequence".

Why it works

Balance is a constraint on selection frequency, which an additive offset can steer without altering the learned scoring. Ablations:

  • No bias → skew persists (demo: MaxVio ≈ 2.57 at u=0).
  • Gate from the biased score → bias leaks into outputs and the model must learn around it.
  • Drop node-limit M=4 → balance fine, cross-node all-to-all grows.
  • Big-α aux loss → balance but worse perplexity. Wang: Loss-Controlled MaxVio 0.72 / 0.52 vs Loss-Free 0.04 / 0.04 at 1B / 3B.

Where it breaks

  • Collapse loop: popular expert → more gradient → better → more popular.
  • Aux-loss dilemma: small α collapses, large α erases specialization.
  • Micro-batch bug (Qiu et al. 2025): per-micro-batch LBL over 1–4 sequences forces within-sequence uniformity; a code-only sequence is punished for using code experts. Global-batch LBL fixes it.
  • Stragglers: EP all-to-all is a synchronous barrier; one hot GPU stalls all.
  • Inference: no controller at serve time; routing depends on batch composition.
Security. Hayes–Shumailov–Yona 2024 (Buffer Overflow in MoE): cross-batch dependence via capacity lets co-batched queries alter a victim's output. Yona–Shumailov–Hayes–Carlini 2024 (Stealing User Prompts): Expert-Choice routing plus CUDA topk tie-breaking recovers a victim prompt at ~100 queries/token, shown on a two-layer Mixtral.
Misconception. "Experts specialise in domains." Wang–Hayou–Nalisnick 2026 argue routing reflects hidden-space geometry; Mixtral's analysis found mostly syntactic/token-level specialization.

Excluded as unverified: an 85% speedup from dropping 12% of tokens; a 79.2% all-to-all latency share; specific sequence-vs-batch validation-loss numbers from secondary summaries.

In the wild

  • DeepSeek-V3: γ=0.001 then 0.0; sigmoid + normalise; node-limited M=4; α=1e-4 safety net.
  • Mixtral / Megatron default: Switch-style aux loss ~1e-2, softmax over top-k logits.
  • Megablocks: experts as one block-sparse matmul; imbalance costs no FLOPs, no drops.
  • vLLM / SGLang: moe_align_block_size sorts token-expert pairs and pads to block multiples before grouped GEMM.
  • OLMoE: dropless token-choice, early routing saturation, low co-activation.

Open problems

  • Balance vs specialization Pareto: no agreed frontier. Batch-wise loosening helps, loss-free matches or beats aux loss, yet strict balance still wanted for EP.
  • Training/serving mismatch: the controller exists only in training; at serve, batch makeup decides load. Decoupling routing from batch composition is unsolved (and is the security issue).
  • Router learnability: top-k selection is non-differentiable; ReLU/Dirichlet/OT trade stability for gradient. No sample-complexity theory.
  • Optimal granularity beyond small scale.
  • Why obvious fixes fail: bigger α kills specialization; per-sequence balance forces uniformity on non-uniform data; expert-choice balances but breaks batch independence.

Lineage

Jacobs et al. 1991 mixtures of experts → Shazeer 2017 noisy top-k + importance/load CV² → GShard 2020 (top-2, capacity) → Switch 2021 (αNΣfP) → ST-MoE 2022 (z-loss) → Expert Choice 2022 → DeepSeekMoE 2024 (fine-grained + shared) → Loss-Free Balancing / DeepSeek-V3 2024 → global-batch LBL and differentiable routers 2025–26.
