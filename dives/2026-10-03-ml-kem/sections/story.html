A shared secret is 256 bits, and each bit is a clock hand on a dial of 3,329 ticks. A 1 sets the hand to 6 o’clock (tick 1665). A 0 sets it to 12 o’clock (tick 0).

The naive way. With no noise, the hands are exact. Anyone who copies them can solve the system with Gaussian elimination, and the key falls out.

The key idea: smudge. Each hand hides under random noise of a few hundred ticks. Eavesdroppers see only the smudge, and sorting out the hands means solving a noisy lattice problem.

The receiver cleans up. Its secret removes the big term, which cancels exactly. What remains is a small leftover with σ ≈ 76 ticks, far under the 832-tick mistake edge.

The safe margin. A bit flips only if the leftover crosses a dashed edge at 832 ticks. At σ ≈ 76 that is about eleven standard deviations away.

Rounding costs the most. The sender keeps only 4 bits of v, so each value snaps to one of 16 levels. That rounding supplies 62% of the noise variance, the biggest single term.

Tampering gets no help. Flip one bit of the ciphertext and the receiver re-encrypts to check it. The check fails, and the decapsulator returns a pseudorandom key with no error, so the attacker has no reject signal to learn from.
