Sequence CRDTs for collaborative text: RGA, Fugue and Eg-walker

A sequence CRDT gives every character a permanent ID so concurrent edits merge without a server. The hard parts are the tie-break (a bad one weaves two typed words together) and metadata (168 kB saved for 105 kB of text). Eg-walker keeps only an edit log and builds a CRDT just for the concurrent stretch: 23.5 ms on a trace that took textbook OT 61 minutes.

Why it matters: offline-first and peer-to-peer editors depend on this. Yjs, Automerge, Loro and Zed all ship a version of it, and the cost of getting it wrong is unreadable text or a bloated file.

The whole idea in 9 scrolls

How two offline copies of a note merge with no server, and the 2024 trick that took one real merge from an hour to milliseconds

Two people hold the same note, Helo, and edit it offline. No server decides who goes first. When they reconnect, both copies must end up identical.

The obvious way: positions. User 1 inserts an l at index 3. User 2 adds ! at index 4. Swap the edits as-is and User 1 gets Hell!o while User 2 gets Hello!. Same edits, different documents.

The CRDT fix: names, not numbers. Give every character a permanent ID and say “insert after o4”. That sentence means the same on every copy, so both land on Hello!.

A new problem. Now two people each type a whole word right after the same character. “After milk” no longer names a unique spot, so the algorithm needs a tie-break rule.

A tie-break can wreck the text. If ties are broken character by character, the two words weave into ebgrgesad (the example from the Fugue paper). Every copy agrees. Nobody can read it.

Fugue's rule. Hang characters in a tree so that a typed run becomes a subtree, and runs stay whole. In a simulation I ran for this dive, plain RGA wove two backward-typed words into xaybzc; Fugue kept them whole.

The price: nothing is forgotten. A deleted character must stay as a tombstone, because a concurrent edit may still point at it. In the Fugue paper's benchmark a 105 kB document saves to 168 kB (Yjs: 160 kB).

Eg-walker's trick. Keep only the log of edits (an event graph). Most history is a straight line, where no CRDT is needed. Build one temporarily for the stretch where people really overlapped, then throw it away.

The payoff. On a real trace of two weeks of offline editing, merging took 61.1 minutes with textbook OT and 23.5 ms with Eg-walker. Peak merge memory was 8 MiB against Automerge's 271 MiB.

Plain English
In everyday words

Two housemates, one shopping list, no referee

Two housemates photocopy a list and each add a word at the bottom. To merge without a referee, every letter gets a name tag and edits say “put this after tag o4”.

The catch: a careless tie-break can weave the two words together, and the name tags make the file bigger than the text.

Picture a paper shopping list taped to the fridge, and two housemates who each photocopy it before going out. Ana writes eggs at the bottom of her copy. Ben writes bread at the bottom of his. Later they have to tape the two copies back into one list, and nobody is allowed to phone a third person to decide who goes first.

The hard part is that “line 3” means different things on each copy once someone has added a line above it. So instead of numbering lines, give every written character a permanent name tag, and write each addition as “put this right after the character with tag o4”. That instruction means the same thing on every copy.

That fixes one problem and creates another. Ana and Ben both said “right after milk”. Something has to break the tie, and a careless rule can weave the words together letter by letter into ebgrgesad. Every copy would agree on that mess, which is exactly why it is a nasty bug. A good rule keeps each word whole.

An everyday example, start to finish

The list says milk. Offline, Ana types eggs after it and Ben types bread after it. When the copies meet, the app has to place two sets of letters that both claim the same spot. The best answer puts all of eggs and then all of bread (or the other way round), never mixed. The second problem is memory: to stay safe, the app keeps a name tag for every letter ever typed, even ones later erased, so the file grows well past the plain text.

Context
§1 · Prerequisites

Six things to know first

  • Concurrent edits: neither author saw the other's.
  • OT transforms index operations; CRDTs give elements IDs so operations commute.
  • Tombstone: a deleted element kept as a hidden marker.
  • Replication without a leader. Each device keeps a full copy and edits locally; changes travel later, possibly out of order. Goal: strong eventual consistency, meaning replicas that have seen the same operations hold the same state (Gomes et al. define it formally).
  • Happened-before and concurrency. Operation A happened before B if the author of B had seen A. If neither saw the other, they are concurrent. Concurrency is the only case that needs conflict resolution.
  • Operational Transformation (OT). Operations are index-based (“insert x at 5”); concurrent ones are transformed against each other to shift indexes. Google Docs has long used OT with a central server.
  • CRDT. A data type whose operations commute, so replicas converge regardless of delivery order, with no transform step. Sequence CRDTs give every element a unique ID.
  • Tombstone. A deleted element kept as a hidden marker so later operations that refer to it still resolve.
  • Interleaving. Characters of two concurrent runs end up woven together. It is a readability bug, not a convergence bug.
§1b · Real-world context

Behind Yjs, Automerge, Loro and Zed's editor

You pick a library and a transport and exchange binary updates; you watch document size and load time. Figma and (historically) Google Docs are not CRDTs: they order edits on a server.

What it is

A sequence CRDT is the data structure behind real-time and offline collaborative text. It stores a document as a list of characters, each with a permanent unique ID, plus enough metadata to decide where concurrent insertions go. Any replica can edit freely; merging is deterministic.

Why it exists

Before it, collaboration meant a central server serializing every keystroke (Google Docs, built on OT, per Google's 2010 announcement). That fails offline and gets complicated peer-to-peer. Zed's team tried OT in 2017 and chose a CRDT because operations commute and there is no transform step to get wrong.

Where it is used

YjsYATA-derived JavaScript library; the de facto web default. MIT.
AutomergeRust core with JS/WASM wrappers; history-preserving documents. MIT.
LoroImplements Fugue text and Eg-walker; also a rich-text CRDT (per its README).
diamond-typesSeph Gentle's Rust library; the Eg-walker reference implementation.
Zed editorPer its 2022 post: CRDT text with anchors, fragments and tombstones, ordered by Lamport timestamps.
NotionSays its offline pages migrate to a “new CRDT data model”; the text algorithm is not stated.
Not CRDTs: Figma is server-authoritative with last-writer-wins per property (“Figma isn't using true CRDTs though”), and says simultaneous edits to the same text value don't work. Google Docs is OT with a central server (2010 source; current internals not verified).

How you use it

// Yjs: a shared text, synced by exchanging updates
import * as Y from 'yjs'
const doc = new Y.Doc()
const text = doc.getText('note')
text.insert(0, 'milk')
const update = Y.encodeStateVector(doc)       // what I have
// ... send/receive binary updates over any transport:
// Y.applyUpdate(otherDoc, updateBytes)

// Loro (from its README): export a delta, import it elsewhere
const docA = new LoroDoc(); const listA = docA.getList("list")
listA.insert(0, "A")
const bytes = docA.export({ mode: "update" })
docB.import(bytes)                // docA and docB now converge

You never touch IDs or tie-breaks. You choose a library, a transport (WebSocket, WebRTC, files) and a persistence strategy, and watch document size and load time.

When to use it, when not

Use a text CRDT when users edit offline or peer-to-peer, when you want local-first apps, or when you can't afford a single ordering server.
Skip it when one service owns the document and is always reachable: server-ordered OT or last-writer-wins on fields (Figma's choice) carries far less metadata.

Who should care

Frontend and app engineers building editors or notes; infra engineers sizing sync backends; and anyone building local-first software, where document size and cold-load time decide whether the app feels fast.

Technical deep dive
§2 · The one-liner

&ldquo;Unique IDs make all copies converge&rdquo; hides five things

True, but convergence is the easy part. The tie-break decides readability, tombstones never leave, and metadata outweighs the text. Five hidden things the rest of the dive answers.

“A sequence CRDT gives every character a unique ID, so ‘insert after this character’ means the same thing on every replica and all copies converge.”

True, and it hides five things:

  1. Convergence is the easy part. The hard part is the tie-break for two inserts after the same character. A rule can converge perfectly and still weave two typed words together.
  2. Deleted text is never really gone. A tombstone must stay, because a concurrent insert may name it as its anchor.
  3. The metadata is bigger than the text. In the Fugue paper's benchmark, a 105 kB document saves to 168 kB.
  4. You may not need the CRDT most of the time. Eg-walker keeps only an edit log and builds a CRDT just for the stretch where people truly overlapped.
  5. “Same text everywhere” is not “what the users meant.” Convergence does not preserve intention; the interleaving literature exists because of this gap.
§3 · The problem

Positions shift, OT goes quadratic, CRDTs are heavy

Guess first A 105 kB document is saved with full CRDT metadata (Fugue paper). How big is the file?

168 kB for Fugue (Yjs: 160 kB). The metadata is about 60% of the text's size. Nothing is forgotten: deleted characters stay as tombstones.

Index edits shift each other (Helo becomes Hell!o). OT merges diverged branches in quadratic time: 61.1 min on one real trace. CRDTs avoid that but carry an ID per character and a lot of RAM.

Forcing constraint: with no server to order operations, every replica must independently compute the same order for concurrent inserts. That order is decided by a deterministic rule, and the rule's shape decides both correctness and readability. A second constraint pulls the other way: to keep resolving future references, deleted elements cannot simply be forgotten.

§4 · Mental model

A shelf where books say &ldquo;put me after book 7731&rdquo;

Barcodes make “after 7731” mean the same on every copy. The picture breaks in three places: ties, tombstones and the lack of a head librarian.

Picture a library shelf where every book has a barcode. Nobody says “put it at slot 40”; they say “put it right after the book with barcode 7731.” Two librarians can work on separate copies of the shelf plan and later merge, because “after 7731” has the same meaning on both. The reading order of the shelf is the document.

Where the picture breaks:
  • Two librarians may both shelve a run of books right after 7731. A shelf can't hold both at the same spot, so a tie-break rule decides the order, and a bad rule alternates their books one by one. Real shelves never do that.
  • If a librarian discards book 7731, a colleague who was offline may still say “after 7731.” So an empty bookmark (a tombstone) must stay on the shelf forever. Real librarians would just remove it.
  • Shelf order is global in a library. Here every librarian must derive the same order independently, with no head librarian.
§5 · The mechanism, traced

Fugue's tree keeps typed runs whole; Eg-walker replays only the overlap

Guess first Two users each type a three-letter word backwards at the same spot of XZ (A: abc, B: xyz). Plain RGA produces:

XxaybzcZ. Every insert has the same anchor, so the descending-ID rule alternates the two users' characters. The replicas converge; the text is just unreadable. Fugue's tree gives XabcxyzZ (my simulation, in the hands-on section).

Trace one example under RGA and Fugue, then step through Eg-walker's replay with two states per character (prepare and effect). Step-through inside.

1 · From a position to a name

Take the Eg-walker paper's own example. Two users start from Helo. User 1 inserts l at index 3. User 2 inserts ! at index 4. Applying User 2's operation unchanged on User 1's machine gives Hell!o. The index has to become 5. A sequence CRDT removes the problem at the source: each character has an ID (H1 e2 l3 o4), and User 2's operation is “insert ! after o4”. It needs no adjusting.

2 · Same spot, two writers: RGA vs Fugue, traced

Start from the shared text XZ. User A types abc backwards (insert c at index 1, then b at index 1, then a at index 1). User B does the same with xyz. This is the sequence people use when they build a list by repeatedly adding at the top. I wrote a small simulation for this dive (seq_crdt_sim.py) and ran it; the traces below are its output.

RGA. Every insert says “after X” and carries an ID (counter, replica). Ties go to the larger ID first (the Fugue paper's appendix describes RGA's order as descending by timestamp, which my simulation follows). A's IDs are (3,A) c, (4,A) b, (5,A) a; B's are (3,B), (4,B), (5,B). Sorted by descending ID: x(5,B) a(5,A) y(4,B) b(4,A) z(3,B) c(3,A), so the document is XxaybzcZ. Every character is individually correct, and the words are woven.

Fugue. Characters hang in a tree. To insert at an index, take the character before it as the left origin. If the left origin has no right child, the new node becomes its right child; otherwise it becomes a left child of the next element in the traversal (the right origin). The list is the in-order walk: left children, the node, then right children; same-side siblings are ordered by ID (quoted from Section IV of the paper).

Here, X already has a right child Z, so c becomes a left child of Z; b sees X has a right child and becomes a left child of the next element, which is c; a does the same under b. A's run is a chain of left children, and B's is another chain under Z. A subtree is contiguous in an in-order walk, so each run stays whole: XabcxyzZ.

3 · Eg-walker: keep the log, replay only what overlapped

Eg-walker flips the design. Each replica keeps the event graph: every edit as an event with its parents (what the author had seen). The current text is stored as a plain string. A CRDT is built only while merging, over the stretch of history that is actually concurrent, and then discarded.

To transform an event, the algorithm needs to interpret it at the version its author saw, while applying it to the version that exists now. So the temporary CRDT tracks two states per character at once: s_p in the prepare version and s_e in the effect version. Three operations move between versions: apply, retreat (remove an event from the prepare version) and advance (add it back). Step through the paper's example below.

Two optimisations make it fast. If an event's version and its parent's version are both critical versions (every earlier event is an ancestor, every later one a descendant), nothing is concurrent there, the transformed event equals the original and the internal state can be thrown away. And a merge only replays from the latest critical version before the new events, so the work scales with the concurrent stretch, not the whole history.

§6 · The math

Order is an in-order tree walk; merging costs O((k+m) log(k+m))

Siblings sorted by ID make order depend only on the set of nodes. A subtree is contiguous in the walk, so runs stay whole. Eg-walker's worst case is O(n² log n).

Order as a function of the set of operations

order(n) = order(L1) ⧺ … ⧺ order(Lp) ⧺ [n] ⧺ order(R1) ⧺ … ⧺ order(Rq)→ in plain terms: a node's list is its left subtrees, then itself, then its right subtrees, where siblings on the same side are visited in ID order (Fugue, Section IV).

Why this form? Convergence needs the order to depend only on the set of nodes (each carrying parent, side and ID), never on arrival order. Sorting siblings by a total order on IDs does exactly that: two replicas holding the same nodes build the same tree and walk it the same way. Inserts commute because adding a node never changes the relative order of nodes already placed.

Why a subtree gives non-interleaving

An in-order walk emits a subtree's nodes as one contiguous block. If a typed run forms one subtree, concurrent insertions elsewhere can land before or after the block but not inside it. Fugue's definition 4 (“maximal non-interleaving”) states this precisely: if A is the left origin of B and B appears earlier in the list than any other element with left origin A, then A and B are consecutive; the mirror condition holds for right origins with exceptions; and ties between equal origins go to the lower ID.

RGA's rule

place e after e.prev, then skip while next.id > e.id, id = (lamport, replica)→ in plain terms: land right after your anchor, but step over any neighbour with a newer stamp; the result is concurrent siblings in descending ID order.

This compares IDs only, never where in a typed run a character came from, which is why backward typing (same anchor for every character) interleaves while forward typing (each character anchors to the previous one) does not.

Cost of merging in Eg-walker

T(k, m) = O((k + m) · log(k + m))    worst case O(n² log n)→ in plain terms: two offline branches of k and m events cost a few log-sized tree lookups per event; only an adversarial graph shape makes it quadratic.

From the paper: each apply, retreat or advance touches balanced trees holding at most 2(k+m)+1 entries, so each costs O(log(k+m)) and there are O(k+m) of them (apply k, retreat k, apply m, advance k). For an arbitrary graph of n events, each event may retreat or advance up to n earlier ones, giving O(n² log n); the paper says this case is unlikely in practice and that a poor traversal order made one trace as much as 8× slower to merge.

The state machine

sp ∈ {NIY, Ins, Del 1, Del 2, …}   se ∈ {Ins, Del}→ in plain terms: s_e says whether the character exists in the latest document; s_p says whether it existed, and how many deletions have hit it, at the version the current event was written against.

Why Del n and not just Del? Two concurrent users may delete the same character. Retreating one deletion must not resurrect the character while the other still applies, so the prepare state counts deletions.

Lower bound (not verified): a search snippet attributes to Attiya et al. a result that metadata must grow at least linearly with deleted elements for a large class of list protocols. I did not read the paper, so treat it as a pointer, not a claim.
§7 · Why it works

Identity turns &ldquo;where&rdquo; into a relationship every replica resolves alike

Guess first Eg-walker clears its internal state at critical versions. On trace S1 (sequential editing), how much slower is merging without that optimisation?

About 5×: 1.8 ms with it, 9.8 ms without (paper Fig. 9). On A2, which has no critical versions, it makes no difference (23.5 vs 23.7 ms).

Remove any piece and something specific breaks: indexes shift, replicas diverge, anchors vanish, runs weave, or merge time balloons. Ablation table inside.

The core insight: concurrent edits only conflict about where. Give every character an identity, and location becomes a relationship between identities that every replica can resolve identically. The remaining design freedom is the tie-break, and that single rule decides both convergence and readability.

Knobs a practitioner touches

  • Garbage collection (Yjs doc.gc): on, old content of deleted items is dropped; off, you can restore old versions but the document only grows.
  • Run-length encoding. Storing a typed run as one span cut Yjs's entries from ~180k to ~12k on the benchmark trace (Seph Gentle's post).
  • Cached document text (Eg-walker): store the final text next to the event graph so opening a document needs no replay. Fig. 8's “cached load” bars show 0.01–0.12 ms across all seven traces.
  • Traversal order (Eg-walker): a poor order slowed one trace by up to 8×; the topological sort prefers shorter branches first.
  • Tie-break detail (Fugue vs FugueMax): FugueMax visits right-side siblings in reverse order of their right origins; Fugue uses ID only. They differ only where some interleaving is unavoidable.
Why Fugue still matters if Eg-walker exists: Eg-walker needs some rule for concurrent insertions inside its temporary state. Its authors use a Yjs-style rule and conjecture that it is maximally non-interleaving; Fugue is where the property is defined and proved for a CRDT.
§9 · Where it breaks

Weaving, tombstone growth and metadata bloat

Guess first One user deletes a character. Another, offline, inserts text right after it. Why can't replicas just erase the deleted character?

The offline insert says “after character c”. If c were erased, that instruction would have nothing to attach to. The Fugue paper notes tombstones may be ancestors of live or concurrent nodes.

Interleaving (even the 2019 paper that named it had errata), tombstones GC can't safely drop, metadata near 60% of the text, and Eg-walker's limits: plain text only, O(n² log n) worst case.

Interleaving, forward and backward

The Fugue paper's Figure 1 shows the classic case: after a shared “milk” line, one user types eggs and another bread at the same spot, and a weak algorithm yields ebgrgesad. Its Figure 2 shows a semantic version: two users each add items at the top of a list and one user's item ends up under the wrong heading. Per the paper's Table I, Logoot interleaves in forward, backward and multi-replica cases; RGA is forward non-interleaving but interleaves backward; Yjs shows backward interleaving in multi-replica cases.

Published results have been wrong here too. The 2019 PaPoC paper (Kleppmann, Gomes, Mulligan, Beresford) now carries errata: its non-interleaving definition cannot be satisfied by any algorithm, and its proposed algorithm does not converge. The Fugue paper is the fix. Even the paper that named the bug had a bug.

Tombstones and garbage collection

A tombstone can't simply be deleted because later (or concurrent) operations may name it. Removal is safe only once the deletion is causally stable, meaning every replica is known to have seen it. Bauwens and Gonzalez Boix (MPLR 2020) work on this, but it requires knowing the full replica set. Practitioners report Yjs documents trending upward even with GC on, and that purging history breaks mergeability (forum threads; secondary evidence).

Metadata cost

From the Fugue paper: saved metadata is about 60% of the plain text's size (168 kB file for 105 kB of text), and memory is roughly 23 bytes per character, or 13 bytes per character counting tombstones. The simple variants of Fugue and FugueMax cost 618 and 685 bytes per character, which is why implementation engineering (run-length encoding, B-trees) matters more than the paper's pseudocode.

Eg-walker's own limits (from its paper)

  • Plain text only. Rich text, spreadsheets and the like are stated future work. The paper notes that a critical-version cut does not hold for Peritext because of how it processes concurrent annotations.
  • Worst case O(n² log n), and traversal order matters (up to 8× slower on one trace).
  • Benchmarks are single-node with artificial latency; the async traces were reconstructed from Git history, not keystrokes.
  • Maximal non-interleaving is a conjecture for its tie-break, not a theorem.

Rich text, move, undo, cursors

Formatting is its own conflict class. The Peritext essay lists overlapping bold/italic, conflicting highlight colors and insertion at the edge of a link (Word, Google Docs and Pages disagree on whether new text at a link's end is linked). Peritext covers inline marks only; block elements are follow-up work. I did not research undo, cursor stability or move operations in depth.

Misconception: “converged means correct.” Convergence only says all replicas agree. The PaPoC paper's framing is that agreement alone does not make an editor usable.
Misconception: “CRDTs beat OT everywhere.” The interleaving problem affects both (Fugue abstract), and OT with a central server carries much less metadata. The real trade is metadata and offline support against server simplicity.

How production systems deviate from the textbook

  • Yjs: a modified YATA; the lean-yjs verification project says the original YATA pseudocode contains errors. Stores runs of typed characters as single structs and partially collects tombstones.
  • diamond-types: a range tree (B-tree) instead of a linked list, a separate rope for document text, and fixed blocks of 32 entries per leaf. Recording deletion time would double memory (1.12 MB to 2.34 MB), so the published benchmarks use the Yjs-style deleted flag.
  • Zed: insertions are immutable and edits split them into fragments; deletes hide fragments and carry a version vector so they skip concurrent insertions; concurrent inserts order by descending Lamport timestamp with a replica-ID tie-break; undo is a per-operation counter.
  • Figma: not a text CRDT at all. The server holds the latest value per property per object; last write wins.
Research edge
§11 · Frontier

Eg-walker and Fugue are the 2024&ndash;25 advances

Eg-walker (EuroSys 2025): 23.5 ms vs OT's 61.1 min, with memory 1–2 orders lower. Fugue (TPDS 2025): proves maximal non-interleaving. Four newer pointers, flagged by how much I verified.

Status of the research, honestly: the two big results here are recent and verified from primary sources. The 2026 items below I only saw as abstract pages and say so.

1 · Eg-walker (Gentle & Kleppmann, EuroSys 2025, arXiv Sept 2024)

Problem. OT merges diverged branches too slowly; CRDTs load slowly and eat memory. Method. Keep the event graph, reconstruct state with a temporary CRDT over only the concurrent stretch, and track prepare and effect states per character (section 5). Results (paper, Fig. 8, 10, 12; Ryzen-class desktop, single core):

  • Merge on trace A2 (299 authors, 698 k events, mean concurrency 6.11): Eg-walker 23.5 ms, OT 61.1 min, reference CRDT 26.2 ms, Yjs 74.2 ms, Automerge 520 ms.
  • Sequential traces S1–S3: 1.8–3.6 ms, about as fast as OT and roughly an order of magnitude faster than the reference CRDT.
  • Memory while merging: Eg-walker peak 4.7–79.5 MiB, but steady state 73 KiB–1 MiB. Yjs 19.5–30.3 MiB, Automerge 241–848 MiB, OT peak up to 6.8 GiB on A2.
  • File size (deleted text omitted, as Yjs does): Eg-walker is smaller than Yjs on sequential and asynchronous traces (e.g. A2: 330 KiB vs 506 KiB) and larger on the two concurrent ones (C1: 981 KiB vs 845 KiB).

Limits. Plain text only; worst case O(n² log n); concurrent traces use artificial latency; the OT baseline is a simple TTF-style algorithm, not a production one; the maximal-non-interleaving property is conjectured.

2 · Fugue and FugueMax (Weidner & Kleppmann, IEEE TPDS 2025, arXiv 2305.00583)

Problem. Interleaving had no precise definition, and the 2019 attempt was itself flawed. Method. Define maximal non-interleaving, introduce a tree-based algorithm whose FugueMax variant provably satisfies it. Results. Fugue is about 94,000 ops/s (11 µs per operation) with 2.4 MB memory on the editing trace versus Yjs 39,000 ops/s and 3.3 MB; saved size is 168 kB vs Yjs 160 kB for 105 kB of text.

§11b · Open problems

Six things nobody has settled

Is Eg-walker's tie-break maximally non-interleaving? Safe tombstone GC? Rich-text blocks? Undo, move, trust.

  • Is Eg-walker maximally non-interleaving? Its authors conjecture yes for the Yjs-style tie-break and leave the analysis to future work. Why hard: Fugue's proof is for a tree-structured algorithm; the YATA-style rule orders via origins in a flat list with different invariants. Who: Gentle, Kleppmann, Weidner.
  • Bounded metadata and safe garbage collection. Tombstones may be ancestors of live nodes. Causal-stability approaches (Bauwens & Gonzalez Boix, MPLR 2020) need to know the replica set, which open peer-to-peer systems don't have. Yjs users report documents that only grow even after deletion.
  • Rich text and blocks. Peritext handles inline formatting; headings, lists and tables are open. The Eg-walker authors say their clearing step doesn't hold for Peritext, so combining a log-based design with rich text needs new ideas.
  • Undo, move and cursors. Moves that survive concurrent edits (Da & Kleppmann), undo with convergent semantics (Stewen & Kleppmann for registers), and stable remote cursors are active topics I did not research deeply.
  • Intention beyond order. Two users rewrite the same sentence: both rewrites survive and the merged text is gibberish. Convergence has no notion of semantic conflict.
  • Trust. What if a replica lies? Byzantine-tolerant CRDT work (e.g. the 2026 Brocco preprint) and verified access control for local-first systems are early.
Disagreement to know about: a Loro blog post critiques Peritext for lacking the state-independence property that Eg-walker's design relies on (relayed by a research agent; I did not read the post).
§12 · Lineage

Agree (2000s), make it readable (2019&ndash;23), make it cheap (2024&ndash;)

OT (1989) → WOOT/Logoot → RGA (2011) → YATA/Yjs → interleaving anomalies (2019) → Fugue → Eg-walker.

  1. OT (Ellis & Gibbs, 1989; from memory): transform index operations against concurrent ones; the basis of Google Docs.
  2. Early sequence CRDTs: WOOT (2006) and Logoot (2009), both from memory; Logoot-style dense identifiers interleave in forward, backward and multi-replica cases (Fugue Table I).
  3. RGA (Roh et al., “Replicated Abstract Data Types”, J. Parallel Distrib. Comput. 71(3), 2011): insert after an anchor, ties by timestamp, tombstones.
  4. YATA / Yjs (Nicolaescu et al., 2016; from memory): left and right origins; the base of today's most-used library, whose pseudocode a formal verification project later found to contain errors.
  5. Interleaving anomalies (Kleppmann et al., PaPoC 2019): names the bug, later corrected by errata.
  6. Peritext (Litt et al., CSCW 2022): rich-text CRDT.
  7. Fugue (arXiv 2023, IEEE TPDS 2025): defines and achieves maximal non-interleaving.
  8. Eg-walker (arXiv 2024, EuroSys 2025): replay an event graph; a CRDT only where needed.

Pattern: first make replicas agree (2000s), then make agreement readable (2019–2023), then make it cheap (2024–). The cost problem is what Eg-walker attacks by unbundling history from live state.

§14 · Question ladder

Test yourself

18 questions from mechanism to open problems, with answers.

Answer in your head first, then reveal and mark yourself honestly. Marks are saved in this browser and feed the progress ring.

Mechanism
1. On “Helo”, why does User 1 have to rewrite Insert(4,'!') to Insert(5,'!')? How does an ID-based operation avoid this?

User 1 has already inserted an l before index 4, so index 4 now names a different position. An ID-based operation says “after o4”; the ID refers to the same character regardless of what was inserted earlier.

→ related card
2. In Fugue, when does a new character become a right child and when a left child?

Take the character before the insertion point as the left origin. If it has no right child, the new node is its right child. Otherwise the new node becomes a left child of the next element in the traversal (the right origin).

→ related card
3. Eg-walker tracks s_p and s_e per character. What does each mean, and why two?

s_e is the character's state in the latest document; s_p is its state at the version the current event's author saw. The event must be interpreted against s_p but its transformed position must be computed against s_e.

→ related card
4. Why does s_p count deletions (Del 1, Del 2) rather than being a boolean?

Two concurrent users can delete the same character. Retreating one deletion must not make the character visible while the other deletion still applies, so the prepare state counts how many deletions hit it.

→ related card
Why
5. Why does an in-order tree walk keep a typed run contiguous?

A subtree's nodes appear as one unbroken block in an in-order walk. If a typed run forms one subtree, nothing from a different subtree can land in the middle of it.

→ related card
6. Why must same-side siblings be sorted by a total order on IDs?

Convergence requires the order to depend only on the set of nodes, not arrival order. A total order on IDs gives every replica the same sibling order independently.

→ related card
7. Why does RGA interleave backward typing but not forward typing?

Forward typing anchors each character to the previous one, so each user's run is a chain. Backward typing anchors every character to the same element, and the tie-break compares individual IDs, alternating the two users' characters.

→ related card
8. Why do CRDTs need to keep the whole structure in memory, while Eg-walker doesn't?

A CRDT needs its per-character IDs to integrate any future operation. Eg-walker only needs them to merge concurrent events, so it keeps the plain text and an on-disk event graph, building the CRDT during a merge and discarding it.

→ related card
Counterfactual
9. What if tombstones were dropped the moment a delete was applied?

A concurrent insert whose anchor was the deleted character would have nowhere to attach, and different replicas would place it differently. Removal is only safe when the deletion is causally stable.

→ related card
10. What if Eg-walker never cleared state at critical versions?

It would keep a CRDT over the whole history, so sequential traces slow down about 5–7× (S1: 1.8 to 9.8 ms; S3: 3.6 to 24.4 ms) and memory rises.

→ related card
11. Flip RGA's skip rule to ascending order. Do replicas still converge? What changes?

They still converge, since any deterministic total order does. Typed order changes: with the same anchor, later inserts now land after earlier ones, which affects how forward and backward typing read.

→ related card
Adversarial
12. How could a malicious replica break an ID-based CRDT?

By reusing an ID with different content or parents (equivocation), different replicas may keep different versions. Byzantine-resilient CRDT work addresses this; plain sequence CRDTs assume honest replicas.

→ related card
13. What event-graph shape pushes Eg-walker toward O(n² log n)?

A graph where each event must retreat or advance over many earlier events, such as many long alternating branches. The paper says this is unlikely in practice and that a poor traversal order made one trace up to 8× slower.

→ related card
14. Why can no algorithm achieve the 2019 paper's non-interleaving definition?

Its errata state that the definition cannot be guaranteed by any algorithm. Fugue's maximal non-interleaving relaxes it to what is achievable.

→ related card
Connection
15. How is Eg-walker like git rebase?

The paper describes it as rebasing a DAG of operations into one linear history with the same effect, transforming each event so it can be applied in topological order.

→ related card
16. How do version vectors relate to safe tombstone removal?

Causal stability, knowing every replica has seen a deletion, is what makes removal safe, and version vectors or acknowledgements are how replicas learn it. It needs a known replica set.

→ related card
Open
17. Is Eg-walker's tie-break maximally non-interleaving?

Unknown: its authors conjecture yes and leave the analysis to future work. Fugue proves it only for its own tree algorithm.

→ related card
18. Can a log-based design like Eg-walker support rich text?

Open. The authors believe the idea generalises, but note their clearing step doesn't hold for Peritext because of how it processes concurrent annotations.

→ related card
Further
§15 · Go deeper next

Three topics that build on this

Causal broadcast and version vectors, Peritext and rich text, and OT correctness (TP1/TP2).

  1. Causal broadcast and version vectors. Every claim above assumes operations arrive after their causes. How replicas track “who has seen what” is also what makes tombstone GC possible.
  2. Peritext and rich-text CRDTs. Formatting spans anchored to character IDs, and the open problem of block structure.
  3. Operational Transformation correctness. Why transform functions are so hard to get right (TP1/TP2 properties), and why server-ordered OT sidesteps part of the problem.
