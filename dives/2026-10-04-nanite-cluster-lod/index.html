Plain English
In everyday words

Patches swap to coarse stand-ins that nobody can see

A statue of a million triangles is cut into 128-triangle patches, each with cruder stand-ins. Each frame, the GPU asks whether a coarse patch would be visible. If not, it uses the coarse one, and neighbours share border points so nothing cracks.

Explain it simply

Think of a map app. Zoomed out, a city is a grey blob; zoomed in, you see every house. The app does not keep one giant picture and shrink it. It keeps the city cut into small tiles, and for every tile it keeps cruder versions too. You only load the tiles you can see, at just the sharpness your screen can show.

Nanite does this for 3D models. A statue with a million tiny triangles is chopped into little patches of 128 triangles. Each patch has coarser stand-ins. Every frame the GPU asks, for each patch: "if I used the coarse version, would anyone notice on screen?" If not, it uses the coarse one. The trick that makes it work is that neighbouring patches always agree on the exact points along their shared edges, so a coarse patch next to a fine patch still fits like a jigsaw piece, with no gaps.

Everyday example

A game has a stone dragon statue made of about 1,000,000 triangles. Stand 2 metres away: the statue fills the screen, so you need nearly all the detail; the game draws the fine patches facing you and skips the back of the statue. Walk 200 metres away: the statue is a 30-pixel smudge. The game swaps each patch for its coarsest stand-in, maybe a few hundred triangles in total, and nobody can tell. Walk back slowly: patches near the middle of the statue get sharper first, one patch at a time, and no seam ever opens up because the borders between patches never moved.

Context
§1 · Prerequisites

Six ideas to know before the cluster DAG

  • LOD: cheaper mesh versions at distance.
  • Meshlet: a 128-triangle batch handled as one unit.
  • QEM: the edge-collapse error metric.
  • Visibility buffer: store the triangle ID per pixel, shade later.
  • HZB: a depth mip pyramid for occlusion.
  • Rasterization: the GPU turns triangles into pixels; hardware shades pixels in 2×2 quads (needed for texture derivatives).
  • LOD (level of detail): cheaper versions of a mesh used when far away. Classic LOD is a few hand- or auto-made versions per object, switched by distance.
  • Meshlet / cluster: a small fixed-size triangle batch (here 128 triangles) processed as a unit.
  • Quadric error metric (QEM): Garland & Heckbert's way to collapse mesh edges while tracking geometric error (CMU).
  • Visibility buffer: write "which triangle is here" per pixel instead of shading; shade later, once per pixel.
  • HZB: hierarchical Z-buffer, a mip pyramid of the depth buffer holding the farthest depth per region; used for conservative occlusion tests.
§1b · Real-world context

Shipped in Unreal Engine 5 and cloned in open engines

Nanite in UE5, plus meshoptimizer for the build side and Bevy's experimental meshlet renderer. It exists so that triangle count tracks screen pixels rather than scene size. Skip it for translucent, mobile or forward renderers.

What it is

A rendering pipeline (Unreal Engine 5's Nanite, plus open clones) that stores each mesh as a hierarchy of triangle clusters at many detail levels, selects the right cut through that hierarchy on the GPU every frame so that error is ≲1 pixel, and rasterizes the result with a mix of hardware and compute-shader rasterization.

Why it exists

Classic LOD has three costs: artists and tools must author LOD chains per asset; each object pops when it switches level; and the CPU submits a draw call per object, capping scene complexity. Film-quality or photogrammetry assets (millions of triangles each) were unusable in real time. Nanite makes triangle count roughly scale with pixels on screen, not scene size, and moves culling, LOD and draw-building to the GPU.

How it's used

  • In Unreal: tick Enable Nanite Support on a Static Mesh; global r.Nanite 0/1; streaming pool r.Nanite.Streaming.StreamingPoolSize; buffer-size cvars r.Nanite.MaxCandidateClusters (12 B each) and r.Nanite.MaxVisibleClusters (16 B each) (verified against Epic's technical details). Epic also exposes Position and Normal Precision per mesh.
  • Open-source build side, via meshoptimizer. The signatures below are from memory of the public header, so check meshoptimizer.h for your version.

    /*max_vertices*/128, /*max_triangles*/128, /*cone_weight*/0.f);

// 2. simplify a merged group with its border locked (per Nanite group step)

Use it / skip it

Use it
  • Opaque, mostly static, high-poly geometry: scans, sculpts, CAD, rock and architecture kits.
  • Many instances of heavy assets.
Skip it
  • Translucency, and forward or mobile renderers (Nanite needs the deferred pipeline and SM6-class features).
  • Small low-poly meshes, where classic LOD wins. One measured test claims Nanite loses under about 2M triangles per mesh; that is blog-grade evidence (CRDG Japan).
  • Heavily masked or alpha-tested foliage, and large WPO animation (see the "Where it breaks" card).

Who should care

Engine and graphics programmers; technical artists deciding import settings; and ML or vision people doing 3D Gaussian-splat LOD, who are porting the same DAG idea (see the frontier card).

§2 · The one-liner

"Unlimited triangles" hides five hard problems

The hard parts: crack-free neighbours with no synchronisation, a DAG rather than a tree, a software rasterizer for tiny triangles, and the costs of overdraw, masks, animation and streaming.

"Nanite renders unlimited triangles by streaming and LOD-ing 128-triangle clusters at pixel scale." That sentence hides five things:

  1. How can two independently simplified neighbouring groups meet without cracks?
  2. How does the GPU pick a consistent LOD cut with no neighbour communication?
  3. Why a DAG and not a tree, and why do the borders alternate?
  4. Why does a software rasterizer beat the hardware one for tiny triangles?
  5. What does it cost? Overdraw, masked materials, animation, ray tracing and streaming.
§3 · The problem

Triangle count must track pixels, not source size

A 1080p screen has 2.07M pixels, so roughly 2M visible triangles is the most that can matter, whatever the source size. A hardware rasterizer shades 2×2 quads, so a one-pixel triangle wastes at least 75% of its lanes.

  • Source size vs screen size. A source asset has 1M–1B triangles. A 1080p screen has 2.07M pixels. The detail you can actually see is at most about one triangle per pixel, so at most roughly 2M visible triangles, or about 16k clusters, independent of source size.
  • Classic discrete LOD pops when it switches, needs authoring per asset, and costs a draw call and CPU culling per object.
  • Hardware rasterizer inefficiency. Pixels are shaded in 2×2 quads, so a triangle covering one pixel still launches at least 4 pixel-shader lanes. At least 75% of that work is thrown away as helper lanes.
  • The constraint that forces the design. LOD decisions must be local (per cluster, in parallel on the GPU), yet the result must be globally watertight.
§4 · Mental model

A tiled map with pinned borders, and where it breaks

Fine and coarse tiles share the same border points, so they always interlock. The analogy breaks because pinned borders never simplify, so the map must be re-tiled at every level. That is why the structure is a DAG.

A zoomable map cut into tiles with pinned borders. Pins mean the fine tile and the coarse tile share the same border points, so they interlock at any mix of zoom levels.

Where the analogy breaks. Pins make tile borders un-simplifiable: a border stays dense forever unless the next level draws its tile borders somewhere else. So the map must be re-tiled at every zoom level with different cuts. That is why the structure is a DAG, not a tree.
§5 · Mechanism, traced

Locked borders let each cluster pick its own LOD

Cluster error 0.8 cm, parent 1.9 cm. At 25 m the parent projects to 0.71 px, so it is drawn; at 12 m the cluster projects to 0.62 px and the parent to 1.48 px, so the cluster is drawn. Visibility goes into a 64-bit atomic-max buffer.

6-step interactive walkthrough inside.

Offline build (per mesh)

level 0:  T triangles → partition (graph partition on shared edges) → clusters of ≤128 tris
repeat:
  group    : merge 8–32 spatially adjacent clusters          [secondary: UE constants]
  lock     : every edge shared with a DIFFERENT group has both vertices locked
  simplify : QEM collapse inside the group to ~50% triangles (borders untouched)
  record   : group error e_g = max(e_g_raw, max child error)   # monotone
             group bounds = hull(children bounds, own bounds)  # nested
  split    : re-cluster the simplified group into new ≤128-tri clusters
             (these are the group's "parent" clusters)
  next level re-partitions the NEW clusters, so last level's locked borders
  now fall inside groups and get simplified
until one root cluster remains

The result: each cluster has (self error, self bounds) and (parent error, parent bounds). All clusters in a group share the same pair.

STEP 1 / 6

The survivor then passes the frustum test and the HZB occlusion test. Big triangles go to the hardware rasterizer and tiny ones to a compute rasterizer. Both write a 64-bit value to the visibility buffer by atomic max, with depth in the high bits so the nearest surface wins.

A later pass reads the (cluster ID, triangle ID) pair from each pixel, fetches that triangle, interpolates its attributes, and shades each pixel once. Materials are binned by tile so the shading work stays coherent.

GPU traversal. Persistent threads pull BVH nodes from a lock-free work queue. A node that is not small enough to draw pushes its children back onto the queue; leaves are clusters (secondary: elopezr, Karis). Culling runs in two passes (see the "Why it works" card).

Conflict on the bit layout. Sources agree that the visibility buffer is 64 bits per pixel with about a 30-bit depth, a 7-bit in-cluster triangle ID (128 triangles = 27), and a cluster ID in the remaining bits. They disagree on the cluster ID width: one source says 27 bits with a 30-bit depth; another describes a 25-bit cluster ID in a 32-bit word plus a 32-bit depth word. I could not confirm either against the Karis slides.
§6 · The math

Projected error picks the cut, and monotone bounds keep it consistent

Draw a cluster when proj(self) ≤ τ < proj(parent), with τ ≈ 1 px and proj(e,d) = e·H/(2·d·tan(φ/2)). Monotone error and nested bounds mean exactly one crossing per path. The whole DAG costs about 2× the source triangles.

Interactive LOD calculator inside.

QEM collapse cost

For a triangle plane p = (a, b, c, d), with a² + b² + c² = 1, the squared distance of a point v = (x, y, z, 1) to the plane is (pᵀv)² = vᵀ(ppᵀ)v. Each vertex keeps Q = Σ ppᵀ over its planes, a 4×4 symmetric matrix. Collapsing vertices 1 and 2 to a point v̄ costs:

Δ(v̄) = v̄ᵀ (Q₁ + Q₂) v̄ , minimised by solving the 4×4 system with last row (0, 0, 0, 1) [Garland & Heckbert 1997] → in plain terms: each vertex remembers how far it may drift from its old surface planes. Summing the matrices means the errors add without storing the planes, which is why a collapse costs O(1) after setup.

Screen-space error

At distance d, the image plane's half-height is d·tan(φ/2) world units and maps to H/2 pixels. So one world unit is (H/2)/(d·tan(φ/2)) pixels, and an error e at distance d projects to:

proj(e, d) = e · H / (2 · d · tan(φ/2)), d = max(‖cam − c‖ − r, ε) (bounding sphere centre c, radius r) → in plain terms: error shrinks with distance and grows with resolution. Using the nearest point of the sphere makes the estimate conservative, so it is never optimistic.

Selection rule

draw cluster c ⇔ proj(e_self, d_self) ≤ τ < proj(e_parent, d_parent), τ ≈ 1 px → in plain terms: "I am fine enough, and my coarser parent is not." Exactly one cluster on every leaf-to-root path satisfies this.

Why it stays monotone

If e_parent ≥ e_self and bounds_parent ⊇ bounds_self, then d_parent ≤ d_self, so proj never decreases as you go up any DAG path. That gives exactly one τ-crossing per path. Siblings share the parent's numbers, so they all agree. The cut is consistent without anyone talking to a neighbour.

Size accounting

Σ triangles = T(1 + ½ + ¼ + …) = 2T ; depth ≈ log₂(T / 128) ; T = 10⁹ → about 23 levels

Each level keeps about half the triangles, so the whole DAG costs about twice the source mesh. Epic reports 14.4 bytes per input triangle on average. A 1.5M-triangle example takes 19.64 MB, against 148.95 MB for a 4-LOD standard static mesh, a 7.6× saving (verified against Epic's technical details).

§7 · Why it works

Locked borders and monotone error make every decision local

Shared border vertices give watertight meshes with no stitching. Shared group numbers let each cluster decide alone, in parallel. Remove the border lock or the monotone rule and the cut cracks, overlaps or never reaches a single root.

  • Watertight. Locked border vertices are identical at every level, so a fine group beside a coarse group shares exact vertices. No stitching, no skirts.
  • Local decisions. Monotone error, nested bounds and group-shared numbers let the GPU decide per cluster, in parallel, with no synchronisation.
  • Constant work per pixel. Error of about 1 px means about 1 triangle per pixel, whatever the source size.
  • Two-pass occlusion culling (secondary). Pass 1 draws the clusters that were visible last frame, tested against last frame's HZB. It then builds a new HZB from that depth. Pass 2 re-tests everything pass 1 culled against the new HZB and draws the clusters that were wrongly culled. The result is conservative-correct with no CPU readback.
  • Software raster. One wave handles a whole cluster, with a 64-bit atomic write to the visibility buffer and no quad waste. Reports put it up to about 3× faster than hardware for pixel-sized triangles (secondary: reported by Karis and blogs).

§8 · Knobs & alternatives

128-triangle clusters, one pixel of error, and the alternatives

Clusters of 128 triangles fit a 7-bit ID and the wave size. A user report found 256 vertices with 128 triangles beat 64 with 64. Alternatives: discrete LOD, Hoppe's progressive meshes, Quick-VDR, and voxels for aggregates.

  • Cluster size: 128 triangles. It gives a 7-bit triangle ID and is a multiple of the wave size. A meshoptimizer user report found 256 vertices with 128 triangles beat 64 with 64, because rasterization cost dominated (verified).
  • Group size: 8–32 clusters (secondary). Bigger groups simplify better but couple more tightly to streaming.
  • τ ≈ 1 px. A bigger τ is faster but shimmers more. Nanite's visual stability leans on temporal AA.
  • Position precision. A 2P grid, at most 21 bits per axis, trades size against stair-stepping (secondary).
  • Streaming pages. 128 KB pages, with 32 KB root pages (secondary; not stated on the Epic technical-details page I fetched).
  • Alternatives. Classic discrete LOD (cheap, but pops and needs authoring). View-dependent progressive meshes (Hoppe), which cannot be evaluated in parallel per cluster. Quick-VDR's clustered hierarchy of progressive meshes (Yoon et al., 2004) (secondary). Mesh shaders and meshlets without a DAG. Voxels or impostors for aggregates.
§9 · Where it breaks

Foliage, animation and masks defeat the cluster cut

Disconnected parts like leaves and hair do not simplify into planes. Wind and skinning break the static bounds. Masked materials need extra raster bins. Nanite is deferred-only and needs a large streaming pool.

Overdraw and thin geometry. Simplification removes few triangles from layered or holey meshes, so many depth layers must be drawn before occlusion helps. One article claims up to 2× worse than expected (80.lv, blog-grade).
Aggregates (leaves, grass, hair). Disconnected parts do not collapse into a plane, so thin features vanish or stay dense. Epic's answer in the UE 5.8 docs is Assemblies, Voxels and Skinning (verified).
WPO and animation. Cluster bounds are static, so displaced vertices can be culled wrongly and need a max-WPO-distance setting. Animation also invalidates cached virtual-shadow-map pages (forums and blogs, secondary).
Masked materials need separate raster bins and a per-pixel alpha test inside the raster loop, which is costly.
No translucency, and deferred only. Forward VR and mobile are out (Meta docs and an Arm blog, secondary).
Ray tracing. Nanite clusters do not map directly to hardware BLAS. Cluster acceleration structures (RTX Mega Geometry, 2025) are the hardware answer (verified that they exist).
Streaming. An undersized pool thrashes, and fast cameras cause pop-in. An SSD is effectively required (secondary).
Subpixel shimmer. Sub-pixel triangles cause temporal instability and rely on TSR or TAA (Epic thin-geometry docs, secondary).
Misconceptions. "Free LODs": the cost moves to material shading, shadows and memory. "Always faster": measured results are mixed. One forum test found Nanite about 1.5 ms slower than an overdraw-optimised LOD scene; another found 55–58 FPS against 47–49 on an RTX 3090 (secondary).
§10 · In the wild

Real engines lock, quantise and bin far more than the paper

UE5 bit-packs everything and bins materials in compute. Bevy runs a meshlet DAG on hardware rasterization only. meshoptimizer's border-locked roots stall at 5–19 meshlets, never 1, without extra tricks.

  • UE5 deviates from the textbook by quantising and bit-packing everything, and by binning materials in compute (UE 5.4; GDC 2024 "Nanite GPU Driven Materials"). I could not fetch the slides, so these details come from Scthe's notes (secondary). Optional work graphs are also used.
  • Bevy builds the meshlet DAG with meshopt and METIS, and runs two-pass culling at runtime. Hardware rasterization only at the time of the post, so expect worse micro-triangle performance (secondary).
  • meshoptimizer's recipes ask for LockBorder and Sparse. Discussion #750 reports root counts of 5–19 meshlets on Bunny, Dragon and Cliff, never 1 without tricks, because disjoint triangles block further simplification (verified).

§11 · Frontier

The cluster idea is moving into ray tracing and Gaussian splats

CuRast's software rasterizer is 2–5× faster than Vulkan on dense meshes. Virtualized 3D Gaussians and LODGE adapt the hierarchy to splats, where seams are hidden by blending. RTX Mega Geometry brings clusters to ray tracing.

§11b · Open problems

Trees, deforming meshes and ray tracing still break the cut

No accepted coarse representation for foliage keeps coverage and shading. Error bounds must hold for every pose of a skinned mesh. Ray tracing needs LOD consistency across rays, and mobile ports are partial.

  • Aggregate LOD. No accepted way to simplify foliage or hair into a coarser representation that preserves coverage and shading. Voxels are Epic's first production answer (UE 5.8), and they are still new.
  • Deforming geometry. The bounds and the DAG assume rigid shapes, and skinning, WPO and morphs break the error bounds. Skinned Nanite is marked experimental in UE 5.5+ (secondary). It is hard because the error bound must hold for every pose.
  • Ray tracing. Cluster BVHs are rebuilt per frame at about 1 px LOD. Open question: how engines keep LOD consistent between primary and secondary rays, and how they stream and blend LOD for them.
  • Portability. 64-bit image atomics and persistent threads limit Nanite to desktop-class GPUs. Mobile and WebGPU ports (community, such as Scthe's nanite-webgpu) are partial (secondary).
  • Attribute-aware error. QEM weights normals and UVs heuristically, with no perceptual error model tied to shading.
  • Splats vs meshes. A 3DGS splat has no shared border to lock, so seams are hidden by blending (LODGE). A principled analogue is unsettled.
§12 · Lineage

From Hoppe's progressive meshes (1996) to Nanite (2021)

Progressive meshes → quadric error metrics (1997) → Quick-VDR clustered hierarchies (2004) → meshlets and mesh shaders → Nanite (2021) → open clones, GPU-driven materials and cluster ray tracing.

Progressive meshes and view-dependent simplification (Hoppe, 1996–97) → quadric error metrics (Garland & Heckbert, SIGGRAPH 1997) → a clustered hierarchy of progressive meshes in Quick-VDR (Yoon et al., IEEE Visualization 2004) (secondary) → meshlets and mesh shaders (NVIDIA Turing, 2018) (secondary) → Nanite (Karis, SIGGRAPH Advances 2021) → open clones (meshoptimizer, Bevy) → GPU-driven materials (GDC 2024) → cluster ray tracing (RTX Mega Geometry, 2025) → Gaussian-splat cluster LOD (2025).

A frequently cited precursor I did not retrieve is Cignoni et al., "Batched Multi-Triangulation" (2005).

§14 · Question ladder

Test yourself

19 questions from mechanism to open research. Reveal, then mark ✓ or ✗; marks feed your progress ring.

Answer in your head first, then reveal and mark yourself honestly. Marks are saved in this browser and feed the progress ring.

Mechanism
1. What do two adjacent groups at different LOD levels have in common along their border, and why does that prevent cracks?

They share identical vertex positions on the shared edges, locked during both groups' simplification, so the geometry meets exactly whatever each side's density.

→ related card
2. Walk one cluster through the selection test when the camera moves from 25 m to 12 m.

At 25 m: self 0.30 px, parent 0.71 px, both ≤ τ = 1, so a coarser cluster is chosen and X is skipped. At 12 m: self 0.62 px, parent 1.48 px > 1, so self ≤ τ < parent holds and X is drawn.

→ related card
3. What exactly happens in pass 2 of two-pass occlusion culling, and why is pass 1 needed at all?

Pass 1 culls against last frame's (stale) HZB, draws the survivors, and builds a fresh HZB from that depth. Pass 2 re-tests the clusters pass 1 culled against the new HZB and draws the ones now visible. Pass 1 gives a good depth pyramid cheaply, with no CPU readback.

→ related card
4. How does a 64-bit atomic max implement visibility and the depth test at once?

Depth sits in the high bits, so max (with reverse-Z) keeps the nearest sample. The low bits carry the IDs, so the surviving value is the visible triangle.

→ related card
5. Why must every cluster in a group share the same parent error and bounds?

If siblings disagreed, one could be drawn fine while a neighbour in the same group is coarse. Their shared interior vertices are not locked, so cracks or overlaps appear. Shared numbers make the group switch atomically.

→ related card
Why
6. Why 128 triangles per cluster?

It matches the 7-bit triangle ID in the visibility buffer and multiples of the wave size. It is small enough for fine culling and large enough to amortise per-cluster overhead.

→ related card
7. Why a DAG and not a tree?

In a tree, each group's borders would be the same at every level and never simplify. A DAG lets level k+1 re-group clusters so the old borders land inside new groups.

→ related card
8. Why is a software rasterizer faster for tiny triangles, even on slower silicon?

Hardware shades 2×2 quads with per-triangle setup, so a one-pixel triangle wastes at least 75% of its lanes. Compute rasterizes a cluster per wave with tiny loops and atomic writes, with no helper lanes.

→ related card
9. Why use the nearest point of the bounding sphere rather than its centre?

The error bound must hold for the nearest point of the cluster's volume. The centre under-estimates projected error for large clusters.

→ related card
Counterfactual
10. What if you did not lock group borders and stitched afterwards instead?

Stitching needs per-pair logic for every LOD combination, plus runtime neighbour checks. Locking trades simplification quality (dense borders) for zero runtime coordination.

→ related card
11. What if a parent's error could be smaller than its child's?

The parent could be selected together with its child, or neither could be selected. That gives overlaps and holes; in my toy ablation, 50 of 3000 random views broke.

→ related card
12. What if the cluster size doubled to 256 triangles?

Fewer clusters and less culling overhead, but coarser culling granularity and more overdraw waste, and the ID bit budget changes. meshoptimizer's 256-vertex, 128-triangle result hints that larger vertex budgets simplify better.

→ related card
Adversarial
13. Build a mesh that makes Nanite's cut huge at any distance.

Dense layered or disconnected sheets, such as foliage cards and hair. Simplification cannot remove triangles without changing coverage, so the projected error stays small and the cut stays fine.

→ related card
14. How does a wind shader make a leaf disappear at the edge of the screen?

Culling uses the original bounds, but WPO moves vertices outside them, so the cluster is culled while it is still visible. A max-WPO-distance setting expands the bounds to fix this.

→ related card
15. Which streaming pattern thrashes the 128 KB page pool?

A fast camera sweep through high-detail areas that need more distinct pages than the pool holds, which gives an evict-and-reload loop.

→ related card
Connection
16. How does this relate to mip-mapping and virtual textures?

It is the same idea: about one texel or triangle per pixel, streamed on demand with feedback about what is needed. The geometry version has the extra difficulty of staying watertight.

→ related card
17. How is a 3D Gaussian splat LOD hierarchy the same as, or different from, this?

It uses the same hierarchical selection by projected footprint. But splats have no shared borders, so seams are hidden by opacity blending (LODGE) rather than by locked vertices.

→ related card
Open
18. How would you build a proper LOD for a tree canopy that keeps its average colour, silhouette and shadow casting?

Open. It needs a coverage-preserving aggregate representation (voxels, impostors, or a learned model) plus a shadow-consistent proxy. Epic's voxels are one answer.

→ related card
19. Can a forward renderer use a visibility-buffer pipeline without a deferred G-buffer?

Partly. Visibility-buffer forward variants exist in research and in some engines, but Nanite as shipped requires deferred shading, and the mobile gap is still open.
