A statue of about 1,000,000 triangles is far more detail than a screen can show. Nanite cuts it into clusters of 128 triangles, and each cluster is one unit the GPU can keep or replace.

The naive way. Simplify each cluster on its own and the edges stop matching. The red seams are cracks. At level 1, border vertices are 30–50% of all vertices, so the problem is not a corner case.

The fix: lock the border. Take a group of four clusters. Every edge shared with a different group keeps its vertices in place, and only the interior gets simplified.

Then build a parent. The locked group is simplified to about half its triangles and re-clustered. Each cluster stores its own error and its parent’s, and all four children share the same pair.

Far away, the parent is enough. At 25 m the parent projects to 0.71 px, under the 1 px target. The GPU draws the parent and skips the four children.

Move closer and it flips. At 12 m the parent projects to 1.48 px, over the target. The children project to 0.62 px, so the GPU draws them. The test is self ≤ τ < parent.

No seams at any mix. Neighbours at different levels share the same vertices on the locked edges, so fine and coarse pieces meet exactly. No stitching runs at render time.

The trade-off. A locked border never simplifies, so it stays dense. The hatched tiles stand for parts that will not collapse, like leaves, and the cut stays fine there.

What it buys on disk. Epic reports 14.4 bytes per input triangle on average. Its 1.5M-triangle example comes to 19.64 MB against 148.95 MB for a 4-LOD static mesh. Keep scrolling for the cards below.
