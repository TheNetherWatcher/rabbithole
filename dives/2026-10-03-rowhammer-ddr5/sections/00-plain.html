Explain it simply. Computer memory (RAM) is a huge grid of tiny buckets, each holding a little water. A full bucket means "1", an empty one means "0". The buckets are packed so tightly that sloshing one bucket over and over makes a neighbour you never touched slowly leak. If it leaks past half-full before the automatic top-up truck comes around, its value silently flips from 1 to 0. That is Rowhammer: you corrupt data you were never allowed to touch, just by reading your own data very fast, very many times, right next to it.

Why anyone cares: a single flipped bit in the right spot, such as a permission flag, a pointer, or a number in a security check, can hand an attacker control of a machine they share with you. That could be a cloud server, your phone, or a browser tab. It is a software-triggered hardware bug, so you cannot simply patch it away.

Everyday example. Imagine a library with shelves one millimetre apart. You repeatedly yank one book off a shelf and slam it back, thousands of times a second. The vibration is enough that the book on the shelf two rows over slips and falls, even though you never touched that shelf. The librarian (the memory's refresh system) walks the aisles re-shelving everything every 64 ms. Rowhammer is yanking your book fast enough that the neighbour falls before the librarian gets there. The defenses are all variations on "a smarter librarian who watches which books you yank", and attackers keep finding the moments the librarian isn't looking.
