The field has two live fronts: (a) make PRAC practical and secure, and (b) attack PRAC itself. Three papers show each side.

Phoenix (ETH COMSEC and Google, IEEE S&P 2026): CVE-2025-6202

Problem: prior DDR5 work (ZenHammer) got only a lone flip, and DDR5 TRR was assumed sufficient. Method: the authors reverse-engineered SK Hynix's TRR and found that its sampling repeats over a 128-tREFI window, about 8× longer than believed. That leaves lightly sampled sub-intervals. Patterns self-synchronise into those blind spots and re-align after missed refreshes. Result: flips on all 15 tested DIMMs. It was the first end-to-end DDR5 exploit on default settings, with root in about 5 minutes (first flip at about 109 s), and it bypassed on-die ECC. Lesson: sampling is structurally defeatable once its schedule is known. That is the capstone argument for moving to counting (PDF · code).

QPRAC (HPCA 2025): secure and practical PRAC

Problem: naive PRAC mitigation queues are "tardy". MOAT (ASPLOS 2025) showed that counts can reach about 1150 against a threshold of 128 before mitigation lands. Method: an N-entry priority service queue per bank tracks the top-N most-activated rows, which gives provable security bounds even at ultra-low thresholds. Result: it claims JEDEC-compliant security down to T_RH = 71. Limitation: it still pays the per-activation counter cost. It is part of an unsettled design race with MOAT, Chronus, CnC-PRAC, CoMeT, and one-hot counting (arXiv).

PRACLeak (ISCA 2025): the defense is a new side channel

Problem: does counting introduce its own leaks? Method: PRAC's ABO and RFM mitigation actions are observable through memory-access latency. A victim's mitigation events time-modulate an attacker's accesses. Result: a timing channel that leaks secrets, plus availability concerns, since an attacker can force ABOs and cause denial of service. In short, the mitigation machinery is itself exploitable. Follow-ups: AutoPRAC uses model checking to discover PRAC attacks automatically. A CCS 2025 generalisation shows that both sampling-based and counting-based defenses leak through mitigation activity (arXiv).
