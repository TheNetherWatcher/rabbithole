A DRAM bank is a stack of rows. Every bit is a sliver of charge in a tiny capacitor, and that charge leaks. So every row is refreshed within 64 ms. Software assumes that memory you do not own cannot change.

The naive hammer. Read the two rows either side of victim V, again and again. V is never opened. Each read couples a little charge out of V's cells.

Charge drains. Both neighbours add their coupling, so V loses charge from two sides. Its next scheduled refresh can be up to 64 ms away, so nothing puts the charge back.

The sense point. After about 9.6K total hammers, V sits at the decision point between 1 and 0. That is the lowest count measured in 2020, split 4.8K per aggressor.

The flip. Read V and the sense amp returns 0. V was never activated, so a bit changed in a row the attacker never touched. A defense has to refresh V before its charge gets there.

Defense 1: TRR. The chip keeps a short list of hot rows, four here, and refreshes their neighbours early. A small list is cheap, and it works while the aggressors fit in it.

The bypass. Use more aggressors than the list can hold. The fifth row is never tracked, so its victims are never refreshed. TRRespass flipped 13 of 42 DDR4 DIMMs, from all three vendors.

Defense 2: PRAC. Keep an exact count for every row, so there is no list to overflow. When a count crosses the threshold, the neighbours get refreshed. The price is latency, reported at up to about 10–18%.

The headline. Flips hit 110 of 129 DDR3 modules in 2014, and Phoenix flipped 15 of 15 SK Hynix DDR5 DIMMs. Counting is the structural fix, but it is costly and not yet proven safe. The cards below cover the details.
