Rowhammer on DDR5: why in-DRAM mitigations keep failing

Hammering one DRAM row electrically disturbs its neighbours and flips bits nobody touched. The defenses split into two camps. Sampling (TRR) keeps a bounded list of "hot" rows and refreshes their neighbours, and it is repeatedly broken because the list is bounded and its schedule can be learned. Counting (DDR5's PRAC) keeps an exact per-row counter, which is the structural fix, but 2024–26 work shows it is expensive and is itself a side channel.

Why it matters: a single flipped bit, such as a page-table permission, a pointer, or an ML weight, can hand an attacker a machine they share with you. It is a software-triggered hardware bug, so you can't patch it away. Phoenix flipped bits on 15 of 15 DDR5 DIMMs and reached root on a default desktop (CVE-2025-6202).

The whole idea in 9 scrolls

How hammering two rows flips a bit in a row nobody opened

A DRAM bank is a stack of rows. Every bit is a sliver of charge in a tiny capacitor, and that charge leaks. So every row is refreshed within 64 ms. Software assumes that memory you do not own cannot change.

The naive hammer. Read the two rows either side of victim V, again and again. V is never opened. Each read couples a little charge out of V's cells.

Charge drains. Both neighbours add their coupling, so V loses charge from two sides. Its next scheduled refresh can be up to 64 ms away, so nothing puts the charge back.

The sense point. After about 9.6K total hammers, V sits at the decision point between 1 and 0. That is the lowest count measured in 2020, split 4.8K per aggressor.

The flip. Read V and the sense amp returns 0. V was never activated, so a bit changed in a row the attacker never touched. A defense has to refresh V before its charge gets there.

Defense 1: TRR. The chip keeps a short list of hot rows, four here, and refreshes their neighbours early. A small list is cheap, and it works while the aggressors fit in it.

The bypass. Use more aggressors than the list can hold. The fifth row is never tracked, so its victims are never refreshed. TRRespass flipped 13 of 42 DDR4 DIMMs, from all three vendors.

Defense 2: PRAC. Keep an exact count for every row, so there is no list to overflow. When a count crosses the threshold, the neighbours get refreshed. The price is latency, reported at up to about 10–18%.

The headline. Flips hit 110 of 129 DDR3 modules in 2014, and Phoenix flipped 15 of 15 SK Hynix DDR5 DIMMs. Counting is the structural fix, but it is costly and not yet proven safe. The cards below cover the details.

Plain English
In everyday words

A neighbour's bits can flip from your reads alone

Memory bits are packed so tightly that hammering one row leaks charge into its neighbours. Read a row fast enough and a bit you never touched flips before the refresh arrives. Like a librarian beaten by a shelf of books.

Explain it simply. Computer memory (RAM) is a huge grid of tiny buckets, each holding a little water. A full bucket means "1", an empty one means "0". The buckets are packed so tightly that sloshing one bucket over and over makes a neighbour you never touched slowly leak. If it leaks past half-full before the automatic top-up truck comes around, its value silently flips from 1 to 0. That is Rowhammer: you corrupt data you were never allowed to touch, just by reading your own data very fast, very many times, right next to it.

Why anyone cares: a single flipped bit in the right spot, such as a permission flag, a pointer, or a number in a security check, can hand an attacker control of a machine they share with you. That could be a cloud server, your phone, or a browser tab. It is a software-triggered hardware bug, so you cannot simply patch it away.

Everyday example. Imagine a library with shelves one millimetre apart. You repeatedly yank one book off a shelf and slam it back, thousands of times a second. The vibration is enough that the book on the shelf two rows over slips and falls, even though you never touched that shelf. The librarian (the memory's refresh system) walks the aisles re-shelving everything every 64 ms. Rowhammer is yanking your book fast enough that the neighbour falls before the librarian gets there. The defenses are all variations on "a smarter librarian who watches which books you yank", and attackers keep finding the moments the librarian isn't looking.

Context
§1 · Prerequisites

Six DRAM facts the rest of the dive relies on

  • DRAM cell: one transistor, one capacitor. The bit is charge that leaks.
  • Refresh: every row topped up within 64 ms (32 ms on DDR5).
  • HC_first: hammers to the first flip. It falls about 10× per generation.
  • TRR, RFM, PRAC: the three in-DRAM defenses, in order.
  • DRAM cell = 1 transistor + 1 capacitor (1T1C). The bit is the capacitor's charge. Charge leaks, so it must be refreshed periodically. (Kim et al. 2014 §2)
  • Rows and the row buffer. A wordline turns on a whole row of cells onto the bitlines. Sense amplifiers read and restore them into the row buffer. Reading a row is destructive and is immediately rewritten.
  • ACTIVATE / PRECHARGE / REFRESH. ACT opens a row, PRE closes it, REF tops cells back up. The fastest you can reopen the same bank is tRC (about 45–48 ns).
  • Refresh window tREFW = 64 ms (DDR3/4; 32 ms on DDR5), split into REFs every tREFI (about 7.8 µs on DDR3/4, about 3.9 µs on DDR5). A victim row survives only if it is refreshed before it leaks past its noise margin.
  • HC_first = the hammer count to the first bit flip. This is the single most important number here: it has fallen about 10× per generation.
  • TRR / RFM / PRAC are the three in-DRAM defenses, in historical order. This dive is mostly about why the first two failed and whether the third is enough.
§1b · Real-world context

It hits shared hardware: clouds, browsers, phones and GPUs

Seen in multi-tenant clouds, browsers, Android, AMD Zen, and NVIDIA GPUs. One GPU flip dropped an ML model from about 80% to 0.1% accuracy. Practitioners watch ECC error counters and set refresh rates.

What it is, in one paragraph. Rowhammer is a read-disturbance phenomenon in DRAM. Repeatedly activating ("hammering") one row electrically disturbs physically adjacent rows, leaking their charge fast enough to flip bits before the next refresh. It was first shown by Kim et al. (ISCA 2014): disturbance errors appeared in 110 of 129 DDR3 modules, with as few as 139K reads to a row, in every module made in 2012–2013 (paper). It is a scaling defect. It gets worse as cells shrink, so no single vendor can fix it.

Why it exists, and why it is a problem. DRAM density doubles by shrinking cells and packing them tighter. Smaller cells hold less charge, which means a smaller noise margin. A tighter pitch means stronger capacitive and electromagnetic coupling between neighbours. So each generation needs fewer hammers to flip a bit. The defect breaks the most basic guarantee software assumes: that writing memory you don't own cannot change it. Before Rowhammer, memory integrity was taken for granted. Now it is an active, unsolved hardware-security problem.

Where it shows up in real systems:

Cloud multi-tenant serversThe canonical threat model: your VM hammers to flip a neighbour tenant's page-table entry.
BrowsersRowhammer.js triggered flips from JavaScript. No native code needed.
Android / mobileDrammer flipped bits deterministically on phones via the contiguous ION allocator.
AMD ZenZenHammer (USENIX Sec 2024) reached AMD and the first DDR5 flip.
NVIDIA GPUsGPUHammer (USENIX Sec 2025) flipped GDDR6 on an RTX A6000, dropping an ML model's accuracy from about 80% to 0.1%.
SK Hynix DDR5Phoenix (S&P 2026, CVE-2025-6202) got root on a default DDR5 desktop in minutes.

How a practitioner touches it (defensive side). You don't "call" Rowhammer. You observe it and configure around it:

# Watch corrected/uncorrected ECC errors per DIMM (rising CE = marginal cells / pressure)
sudo systemctl enable --now rasdaemon
sudo ras-mc-ctl --error-count
sudo ras-mc-ctl --summary
edac-util -v

Server BIOS exposes a refresh-rate multiplier (1×, 2×, or 4× tREFI). The 2015 industry reflex was to force 2×. Memtest86 ships a Rowhammer test (test 13). JEDEC's modern answer is firmware and controller settings for RFM and, in future, PRAC.

Care about it if

  • You run multi-tenant hardware, a browser engine, or a mobile platform
  • You serve an ML inference service on shared GPUs
  • You design DRAM or memory controllers, or you certify hardware

You can mostly ignore it if

  • It is a single-user, physically secured, non-adversarial box
  • No untrusted code runs on the same DRAM as your secrets
  • The attacker needs to run code on the same DRAM as the victim

Who should care. Cloud and infrastructure security, DRAM and memory-controller designers, browser and OS sandbox authors, ML-serving infrastructure, and hardware-security researchers.

Technical deep dive
§2 · The one-liner

"Hammer a neighbour, add TRR" hides five things

Three physical effects cause it, not vibration. TRR's bounded state is what every bypass exploits. The threshold falls from 139K to about 128. RowPress and Half-Double break the neighbour rule. Counting fixes it, but counting is expensive and leaky.

"Rowhammer flips bits in memory you can't access by hammering an adjacent row; vendors added TRR to fix it."

What that sentence hides:

  1. Why hammering a row disturbs a neighbour at all. It is three distinct physical effects, not "vibration".
  2. TRR is a sampling heuristic with bounded state, and that bound is exactly what every bypass exploits.
  3. The threshold keeps collapsing (139K → 4.8K → projected 128), so a defense that is cheap today is ruinous tomorrow.
  4. Hammering isn't the only trigger. RowPress (holding a row open) and Half-Double (distance two) break the "refresh the immediate neighbour" assumption.
  5. DDR5's real fix is counting (PRAC), not sampling. The 2025–26 literature is busy showing that even counting is expensive and leaky.
§3 · The problem

HC_first fell from 139K to 4.8K, and keeps falling

Guess first How many activations can one bank take in one 64 ms refresh window?

0.3million
0million3million

About 1.36 million. That is 64 ms divided by the 47 ns row cycle (tRC). It is arithmetic from the timing parameters, not a measured figure. Against a threshold of 4.8K, the budget is generous.

A bank admits at most about 1.36M activations per 64 ms window. Each generation needs fewer hammers to flip a bit: 4.8K on LPDDR4, and projected near 128. The defense must catch a row before a threshold that keeps shrinking.

1.36Mactivations one bank can take in one 64 ms refresh window: the attacker's whole budget

Software's bedrock assumption is that memory you cannot name, you cannot change. DRAM scaling broke it. By the 20-nm node, the charge separating "1" from "0" and the spacing between rows had both shrunk to where normal-rate activations of one row leak a neighbour past its margin inside a single 64 ms refresh window.

The constraint that forces every design decision is the activation budget against HC_first. A single bank admits at most about tREFW / tRC ≈ 64 ms / 47 ns ≈ 1.36M activations per refresh window. This is arithmetic, not a paper figure. If HC_first is 139K (DDR3, 2014), you need about 10% of that budget aimed at one victim. That is hard but doable. If HC_first is 4.8K (LPDDR4, 2020), it is trivial. Projected future chips sit near 128. The defense problem is: catch an over-activated row before a threshold that is shrinking out from under you, cheaply, across billions of rows.

Concrete generation trend (Revisiting RowHammer, ISCA 2020, 1580 chips):

GenerationHC_first (old node → new node)
DDR369.2K → 22.4K
DDR417.5K → 10K
LPDDR416.8K → 4.8K

The minimum observed overall was 9.6K total activations (4.8K to each of two aggressors, double-sided). Bit flips per fixed activation count rose about 500× over the same span.

§4 · Mental model

Leaky buckets with a refresh truck, and where that misleads

Each cell is a leaky bucket that a refresh truck tops up every 64 ms. TRR is a spotter who tops up shaken neighbours early. PRAC is a counter on every bucket. The picture fails because the effect is electrical, reaches two rows away, and works by holding a row open.

The leaky-bucket grid with a patrolling top-up truck. Each cell is a bucket slowly leaking. The refresh truck tops every bucket up every 64 ms. Hammering a row shakes its neighbours' buckets so they leak faster. A flip happens when a neighbour empties past half before the truck arrives. TRR is "a spotter riding the truck who notes which buckets you've been shaking a lot and tops those neighbours up early." PRAC is "a counter bolted to every bucket that rings a bell the moment it has been shaken too much."

Where the analogy breaks. The disturbance isn't mechanical vibration. It is three electrical effects: coupling, bridges, and hot-carrier injection. It can reach the bucket two rows away (Half-Double). It can also be driven by holding a bucket open rather than shaking it (RowPress). A mechanical-vibration intuition predicts neither.

§5 · Mechanism, traced

Two rows that are only read leak a victim nobody opened

Guess first In the lowest case measured in 2020, how many total activations on the two neighbours flipped the victim?

9.6K. The minimum across 1,580 chips in the 2020 study, split 4.8K per aggressor, double-sided. The 139K figure is an older 2014 DDR3 read count, not this case.

Aggressors A₁ and A₂ sit either side of victim V, and each activation bleeds a sliver of V's charge. After about 9.6K total activations the next read returns the wrong bit, and V was never activated.

Trace one double-sided disturbance on a DDR4 bank. The victim row is V, and the aggressors are A₁ = V−1 and A₂ = V+1.

Row V    (V)  ──┤ victim  ← coupling + leakage from BOTH sides
Row V+1 (A2)  ──┘

one hammer iteration:
  ACT A1   → wordline A1 high: charge dumped to bitlines, sensed, RESTORED
             (A1's toggle capacitively couples into V's cells, nudges leakage)
  PRE A1   → close A1
  ACT A2   → same from the other side of V
  PRE A2
  … repeat ~4,800× per aggressor before V's next REF …
aggressor (being activated) victim (leaking) idle row
  1. ACT A₁ raises A₁'s wordline. Its cells drive the bitlines, the sense amplifiers latch, and they immediately rewrite A₁ (a destructive read followed by a restore). That is fine so far, because A₁ is intact.
  2. The wordline voltage swing couples into V. Three effects are involved (Kim et al. 2014 §3): (a) capacitive and electromagnetic coupling briefly and partially turns on V's access transistors, bleeding charge; (b) bridges, which are process shorts, offer leakage paths; (c) hot-carrier injection from toggling changes transistor leakiness over time.
  3. PRE A₁, then ACT/PRE A₂. V is now disturbed from the other side too. The disturbance is double-sided because both neighbours' coupling adds.
  4. Each iteration bleeds a sliver of V's charge. V is not being refreshed. Nobody has activated V, and its scheduled REF is up to 64 ms away.
  5. After about 9.6K total activations (the worst DDR4-era chip), V's charge crosses the sense-amp decision point. The next time V is read, the sense amp latches the wrong value. The bit has flipped.

The attacker never issued a single ACT V. Memory integrity was violated purely by activity on V's neighbours.

The RowPress variant (the physics knob)

Instead of fast ACT/PRE cycles, keep A₁ open for a long tAggON. A row held open keeps injecting disturbance without the recovery that a precharge gives. Measured against plain hammering, that is about 17.6× fewer activations at tAggON = 7.8 µs and about 159× fewer at 70.2 µs. A single activation suffices at tAggON ≈ 30 ms (RowPress, ISCA 2023). It is worse when the chip is hot.

§6 · The math

PARA's miss chance decays exponentially, but p must rise

Refresh arithmetic gives tREFI = 7.8 µs and a budget of about 1.36M hammers per window. PARA's miss probability is (1 − p)N, but p must rise as HC_first falls. Counters need about 2·N_ACT/T_RH entries.

Slider inside.

Refresh-window arithmetic

tREFI = tREFW / 8192 = 64 ms / 8192 = 7.8 µs
max ACT per window ≈ tREFW / tRC = 64 ms / 47 ns ≈ 1.36M (derived)→ in plain terms: the attacker's whole budget is about 1.36M hammers. A defense only has to make "aim 4.8K of them at one victim, undetected" impossible.

The 8192 REFs per window (Kim et al. 2014 §2.3) give the refresh interval. The activation ceiling is derived from the timing parameters, not measured.

PARA: the stateless probabilistic defense

On every row close, with probability p the controller refreshes an adjacent row. It keeps no counters and no addresses. The chance that a specific victim is never rescued over N aggressor activations is:

Pmiss = (1 − p)N (or (1 − p/2)N if the coin picks one of two neighbours)→ in plain terms: each hammer is an independent coin flip for "refresh your neighbour now." To dodge rescue for N hammers, every coin has to come up tails, and that probability decays geometrically. With p ≈ 0.001–0.005, Pmiss over a window falls below the hardware hard-error budget of roughly 10⁻¹⁵ at under 0.75% performance cost.

Why this form and not a counter? It needs zero state, and that is the whole point. Its weakness is also in the equation. Pmiss depends on N, the number of hammers you must survive. As HC_first falls, you must raise p, and the overhead climbs.

Graphene: counting with Misra–Gries

To refresh exactly the hot rows, you must identify every row that exceeds a threshold within a window, and do it cheaply. Misra–Gries heavy hitters need only k−1 counters to find every element that occurs more than N/k times in a stream of N items, and each stored estimate never exceeds the true count, so there are no false negatives. Applied to Rowhammer, to catch any row that reaches about T_RH/2 activations among N_ACT activations in a window:

entries > N_ACT / (T_RH / 2) = 2 · N_ACT / T_RH→ in plain terms: you don't need a counter per row. You need one per "slot of hammering budget big enough to matter", which is about 2·N_ACT/T_RH. Storage drops from billions of rows to a few hundred entries per bank, but it still scales inversely with T_RH. As the threshold falls from 1000 to 125, storage balloons from about 207 KiB to about 1.5 MiB per the CoMeT analysis.

The bound is restated in the CoMeT survey section. Confirm the constant against the original MICRO 2020 paper before reusing it.

§7 · Why it works

Sampling overflows, counting does not, so the field moved to counting

TRR keeps a bounded list of candidate rows, so it breaks once an attacker uses more rows than it can hold. PRAC keeps an exact count per row, so nothing overflows. Removing exact counting, or the back-off bound, reopens the attack.

The core insight that divides the whole field is sampling versus counting.

  • Sampling (TRR) keeps a small, fixed set of "hot candidate" rows. It works only if the real aggressors stay in that set. Remove the assumption that an attacker activates few distinct rows, and the tracker overflows, so an aggressor goes unrefreshed. That single property is what TRRespass, U-TRR, Blacksmith, ZenHammer, and Phoenix all exploit.
  • Counting (PRAC) keeps an exact per-row count. It works because there is nothing to overflow and nothing to under-sample. The count is ground truth. Remove exactness, for example with approximate or shared counters, and you reintroduce blind spots.

Ablation

  • Remove refresh entirely, and DRAM loses data in milliseconds regardless of attacks. Refresh is load-bearing.
  • Remove neighbour refresh on detection from TRR, and it is just normal refresh. Flips then happen trivially.
  • Remove exact counting from PRAC, and you are back to TRR's bounded-state failure.
  • Remove the Alert Back-Off window bound from PRAC, and an attacker races activations in after the alert. The residual window of about 180 ns, roughly three ACTs, is the controlled remnant of that risk.
§8 · Design knobs

Only the counting designs are exact, and they cost area and latency

Guess first How much latency does PRAC's counter update add, as reported?

Up to about 10–18%. The cost is a read-modify-write on each precharge. Much of the 2024–26 PRAC work is about clawing that cost back.

Refresh, ECC, PARA, TRR, Graphene, CoMeT, and PRAC each buy protection with a different cost. Only the counting designs are exact, and their storage scales as 1/T_RH. PRAC's per-precharge update adds up to 10–18% latency.

§9 · Where it breaks

Bounded trackers break, and Phoenix took root on default DDR5

Guess first What share of DDR4 DIMMs did TRRespass flip?

13 of 42, about 1 in 3. TRRespass used many-sided hammering on chips from all three vendors. Blacksmith's non-uniform patterns later flipped 40 of 40 DDR4 DIMMs.

A tracker that remembers N rows falls to N+1 aggressors. TRRespass flipped 13 of 42 DDR4 DIMMs, and Blacksmith 40 of 40. Phoenix flipped 15 of 15 SK Hynix DDR5 DIMMs and reached root in about 5 minutes.

TRR: bounded tracking. A tracker that remembers N rows is beaten by N+1 aggressors.

  • TRRespass (S&P 2020): many-sided hammering, with up to about 19 aggressors, overflowed the trackers. It flipped bits on 13 of 42 DDR4 DIMMs across all three vendors (paper).
  • U-TRR (MICRO 2021): used retention failures as a side channel to see exactly when and how many rows TRR refreshes. It bypassed TRR on 45 modules, with up to 99.9% of rows flippable (arXiv).
  • Blacksmith (S&P 2022): non-uniform frequency and phase patterns flipped all 40 of 40 DDR4 DIMMs.

DDR5 TRR: Phoenix (S&P 2026, CVE-2025-6202). SK Hynix's DDR5 TRR samples over a long, complex schedule. The best bypass pattern spanned 128 REF intervals, about 8× the roughly 16 assumed. "Self-correcting synchronisation" re-aligns the pattern after missed refreshes, so the hammers land in the sampler's blind spots. It flipped bits on 15 of 15 SK Hynix DDR5 DIMMs (made January 2021 to December 2024). In the ETH COMSEC results, the first flip came at about 109 s, and root on a default DDR5 desktop followed in about 5 minutes. On-die ECC did not help (ETH COMSEC).

The physics breaks the "refresh the neighbour" assumption too.

  • Half-Double (Google, 2021): disturbance reaches distance two. Heavy access to row A plus a few accesses to the intermediate row B flips row C. TRR's own refresh of B acts as an access that propagates the disturbance. This is an intrinsic silicon property that widens as geometry shrinks (Google).
  • RowPress (ISCA 2023): pressing a row, not hammering it. This is orthogonal to activation-count defenses.
Misconceptions practitioners hold (each one is wrong):
  • "ECC fixes it." ECCploit (S&P 2019) crafts multi-flip words and uses correction timing.
  • "DDR5 is immune." ZenHammer and Phoenix say otherwise.
  • "Server or ECC memory is safe." ECCploit targeted server ECC, and ZenHammer hit AMD server and desktop parts.
  • "It needs physical access." Project Zero did it in 2015 from a sandboxed userland program. Rowhammer.js did it from JavaScript.
  • "TRR solved it." Every bypass above says otherwise.
§10 · In the wild

Real attacks reached browsers, phones, clouds and GPU models

GPUHammer flipped one weight bit and cut a model from about 80% to 0.1% accuracy. The deployed stack of TRR, on-die ECC, and RFM is bypassable on default DDR5. PRAC is not yet in the DDR5 systems Google evaluated.

  • Exploits: Google Project Zero (2015) flipped a page-table entry to gain kernel privilege from an unprivileged process. Drammer was deterministic on Android, Rowhammer.js ran in the browser, and Throwhammer worked over the network via RDMA.
  • GPUHammer (USENIX Sec 2025): targeted GDDR6 on an NVIDIA RTX A6000, which is non-ECC by default, with roughly 500K activations per window. A single weight-bit flip dropped model accuracy from about 80% to 0.1%. Across AlexNet, VGG16, ResNet50, DenseNet161, and InceptionV3, the drops ran from 56% to 80% (arXiv). ECC GPUs such as the H100 and RTX 5090 detect or correct these flips.
  • Deployed defenses today (2025–26): TRR, on-die ECC, and RFM are all shown bypassable on default DDR5 (Phoenix). Google states that PRAC is not yet in the DDR5 systems it evaluated. It is standardised for future revisions, not shipping.
  • 2015 stopgap: Apple, HP, Cisco, Lenovo, and IBM shipped BIOS updates that forced 2× refresh (tREFI from 7.8 to 3.9 µs). It was a partial, costly fix.
Research edge
§11 · Frontier

The live fight: make exact counting cheap, and stop it leaking

QPRAC's priority queue stays secure down to T_RH = 71. PRACLeak shows that the mitigation's own latency can leak secrets. Phoenix's 15-of-15 result is the case for counting. Packing is no longer the question; exactness and leakage are.

The field has two live fronts: (a) make PRAC practical and secure, and (b) attack PRAC itself. Three papers show each side.

Phoenix (ETH COMSEC and Google, IEEE S&P 2026): CVE-2025-6202

Problem: prior DDR5 work (ZenHammer) got only a lone flip, and DDR5 TRR was assumed sufficient. Method: the authors reverse-engineered SK Hynix's TRR and found that its sampling repeats over a 128-tREFI window, about 8× longer than believed. That leaves lightly sampled sub-intervals. Patterns self-synchronise into those blind spots and re-align after missed refreshes. Result: flips on all 15 tested DIMMs. It was the first end-to-end DDR5 exploit on default settings, with root in about 5 minutes (first flip at about 109 s), and it bypassed on-die ECC. Lesson: sampling is structurally defeatable once its schedule is known. That is the capstone argument for moving to counting (PDF · code).

QPRAC (HPCA 2025): secure and practical PRAC

Problem: naive PRAC mitigation queues are "tardy". MOAT (ASPLOS 2025) showed that counts can reach about 1150 against a threshold of 128 before mitigation lands. Method: an N-entry priority service queue per bank tracks the top-N most-activated rows, which gives provable security bounds even at ultra-low thresholds. Result: it claims JEDEC-compliant security down to T_RH = 71. Limitation: it still pays the per-activation counter cost. It is part of an unsettled design race with MOAT, Chronus, CnC-PRAC, CoMeT, and one-hot counting (arXiv).

PRACLeak (ISCA 2025): the defense is a new side channel

Problem: does counting introduce its own leaks? Method: PRAC's ABO and RFM mitigation actions are observable through memory-access latency. A victim's mitigation events time-modulate an attacker's accesses. Result: a timing channel that leaks secrets, plus availability concerns, since an attacker can force ABOs and cause denial of service. In short, the mitigation machinery is itself exploitable. Follow-ups: AutoPRAC uses model checking to discover PRAC attacks automatically. A CCS 2025 generalisation shows that both sampling-based and counting-based defenses leak through mitigation activity (arXiv).

  • MOAT (ASPLOS 2025): secure per-row counting, which exposes queue tardiness (arXiv).

§11b · Open problems

Nobody can yet prove a DIMM is Rowhammer-safe

Is PRAC's 10–18% overhead worth it, and who decides? Where should mitigation live? Vulnerability varies by die and batch, so patterns don't transfer, and no accepted test proves a DIMM safe.

  • Is PRAC enough, and at what cost? Exact counting closes the sampling and undercount holes. But the precharge-time read-modify-write inflates timings (reported up to about 10–18% slowdown), the spec "leaves some questions unanswered", and flips have still been observed on PRAC-capable paths (NDSS 2026 analysis). Verdict: a major advance, not a closed case.
  • The threshold-setting problem. Every counter scheme needs a safe threshold below HC_first. But HC_first keeps falling (139K → 4.8K → about 128 projected). A margin that is safe today may be unsafe at the next node, and setting the threshold low raises false-refresh overhead. No stable safe margin exists.
  • Counting at scale. Exact per-row counters over billions of rows are expensive in area, latency, and energy. Where to store them (in the array, in in-DRAM SRAM, or in the controller) is unsettled.
  • Where should mitigation live? DRAM-only is deployable but has repeatedly failed. The memory controller is one option. OS-cooperative is another. SAFARI's self-managing DRAM and VUSec and MSR's "commodity systems still need software defenses" disagree. There is no consensus.
  • Verification. No accepted method exists to prove a DIMM Rowhammer-safe. TRR was proprietary and unauditable, and "RowHammer-free" labels have proved false. Aging effects are uncharacterised.
  • Supply-chain variation. Vulnerability varies by vendor, die revision, batch, and even within one chip. Patterns don't transfer, which defeats one-size thresholds and certification.
  • Economic misalignment. TRR was optional in JEDEC and shipped as an undocumented black box. VUSec argues that self-interested companies under-invest while end users bear the cost. Standardisation lagged the threat by years.
§12 · Lineage

Twelve years from one bit-flip paper to a default-DDR5 root

2014 Kim et al. flip bits in 110 of 129 DDR3 modules. 2015 Project Zero escalates privilege. 2022 Blacksmith hits 40 of 40. 2024 JEDEC publishes PRAC. 2026 Phoenix becomes the first full DDR5 exploit.

  • Before 2014: DRAM disturbance was known as a reliability curiosity.
  • 2014: Kim et al. (ISCA) name Rowhammer and show it is exploitable. They also propose PARA.
  • 2015: Project Zero weaponises it for a kernel privilege escalation. The 2× refresh stopgap ships, and Rowhammer.js appears.
  • 2016–2018: Drammer (mobile) and Throwhammer (network). ECC is still assumed to be safe.
  • 2019: ECCploit breaks ECC.
  • 2020: TRRespass breaks TRR. Revisiting RowHammer quantifies the HC_first collapse. Graphene introduces counting.
  • 2021: U-TRR reverse-engineers TRR. Half-Double shows distance-two disturbance.
  • 2022: Blacksmith flips 40 of 40 DDR4 DIMMs.
  • 2023: RowPress shows that pressing is not hammering. The SAFARI framing paper appears.
  • 2024: ZenHammer reaches AMD and the first DDR5 flip. JEDEC publishes PRAC in JESD79-5C. CoMeT and MOAT appear.
  • 2025–26: GPUHammer targets GPUs and ML. QPRAC, PRACLeak (defense as side channel), and Phoenix (the first full DDR5 exploit, CVE-2025-6202) follow.
Practice & self-test
§13 · Hands-on

Read the error counters and do the budget math, no hammering

Read ECC counters with ras-mc-ctl, where a rising CE count on one DIMM signals marginal cells. Compute your activation budget as tREFW/tRC and work PARA's miss probability by hand. Nothing here touches memory adversarially.

You don't need to hammer anything. Observe the defenses and do the budget arithmetic instead.

  1. Watch the ECC and RAS counters on a real box (safe, read-only): 
    sudo systemctl enable --now rasdaemon
sudo ras-mc-ctl --summary        # memory controller + CE/UE totals
sudo ras-mc-ctl --error-count    # per-DIMM corrected errors
edac-util -v
    A rising CE count on one DIMM is the observable fingerprint of marginal cells.
  2. Compute your own activation budget. Pick your DIMM's tRC (for example 46 ns) and tREFW (32 ms on DDR5). The budget is about tREFW / tRC. Divide it by a plausible HC_first, say 4800. The quotient is how many independent victims one refresh window could, in principle, threaten. Watch it balloon as you plug in the projected HC_first of 128. The slider in the math section does this for you.
  3. Reason through PARA. With p = 0.002 and N = 50,000 hammers, Pmiss = (1 − 0.001)50000 ≈ e−50 ≈ 2×10⁻²². Now set N, the hammers you must survive, to match HC_first = 128. Ask what p you would need for the same safety, and what that does to overhead.
  4. (Optional, your own hardware only) Memtest86 test 13 runs a Rowhammer check from a bootable stick.
§14 · Question ladder

Test yourself

24 questions across mechanism, why, counterfactual, adversarial, connection, and open. Reveal one at a time, or all at once.

Answer in your head first, then reveal and mark yourself honestly. Marks are saved in this browser and feed the progress ring.

Mechanism
1. What are the three physical effects by which activating a row disturbs a neighbour?

Capacitive and electromagnetic coupling between adjacent wordlines, which partially turns on neighbour transistors. Conductive bridges or shorts. Hot-carrier injection, which alters transistor leakiness over time.

→ related card
2. Why is reading a DRAM row destructive, and what restores it?

Sensing dumps the capacitor's charge onto the bitline to detect it, which drains the cell. The sense amplifier simultaneously rewrites the charge back. That rewrite is the restore.

→ related card
3. Why is double-sided hammering worse than single-sided?

The coupling from both bracketing aggressors (V−1 and V+1) adds, so the victim loses charge from two sides. That means fewer total activations are needed to cross the margin.

→ related card
4. How does RowPress differ physically from classic hammering?

RowPress keeps the aggressor row open for a long tAggON instead of rapidly toggling it. The cumulative leakage injected while the row is open replaces many ACTs. That takes one to two orders of magnitude fewer, and it is worse when hot.

→ related card
5. What makes Half-Double reach a distance-two row?

Coupling is not strictly nearest-neighbour. Heavy access to a distance-two row plus a few accesses to the intermediate row propagates the disturbance. TRR's own refresh of the intermediate row acts as an extra access.

→ related card
Why
6. Why does HC_first fall with each process node?

Smaller cells hold less charge, which shrinks the noise margin. They also sit at a tighter pitch, which strengthens coupling. So less injected disturbance is enough to flip a bit.

→ related card
7. Why did the field move from sampling (TRR) to counting (PRAC)?

Sampling keeps bounded state, so it can be overflowed or dodged once the schedule is learned, as TRRespass and then Phoenix showed. Counting is ground truth, and it has nothing to overflow.

→ related card
8. Why is PARA stateless, and why is that both its strength and its weakness?

PARA stores nothing. It is just a coin flip on each row close. The strength is zero area and zero state. The weakness is that it gives only a statistical bound, and the required p rises as HC_first falls, which raises overhead.

→ related card
9. Why does Graphene need only about 2·N_ACT/T_RH counters instead of one per row?

You only need to catch rows that exceed about T_RH/2 activations within N_ACT total. Misra–Gries guarantees that with counters proportional to the stream divided by the threshold, far fewer than one per row.

→ related card
10. Why doesn't on-die ECC stop Rowhammer, and why can it make detection harder?

On-die ECC is a reliability code, not an adversarial defense. Multi-flip words defeat SECDED. By silently correcting isolated flips, it also hides the onset of corruption from monitoring.

→ related card
Counterfactual
11. If HC_first stabilised at 50K forever, which defenses would suddenly be good enough?

Increased refresh and PARA with a modest p would be cheap and sufficient. The crisis is driven by HC_first collapsing, so without that collapse the cheap defenses would hold.

→ related card
12. Remove the ABO back-off window bound from PRAC. What attack reopens?

The attacker races a burst of activations in after the alert and before mitigation lands. The bounded window of about 180 ns, roughly three ACTs, exists to cap exactly that.

→ related card
13. If trackers had unlimited entries, would TRR be secure, and what would it cost?

Yes in principle, but one entry per row means billions of counters. The area, latency, and energy cost is precisely why counting was avoided until PRAC's optimised forms.

→ related card
Adversarial
14. How does Phoenix stay synchronised with a 128-tREFI sampling schedule?

It reverse-engineered the period. It then uses self-correcting synchronisation to re-align its hammers into lightly sampled sub-intervals after any missed refresh.

→ related card
15. Why does many-sided hammering defeat a bounded tracker?

The tracker holds N candidates. N+1 distinct aggressors guarantee that at least one real aggressor is never selected for neighbour refresh.

→ related card
16. How does ECCploit turn error correction into an oracle?

A corrected single-bit read takes measurably longer. Timing it reveals which flips the code absorbed, and that guides the attacker toward an undetectable multi-flip word.

→ related card
17. How could a mitigation mechanism (PRAC) itself leak secrets?

PRAC's mitigation actions, the ABO and RFM stalls, change memory latency in a way an attacker can observe. The attacker times their own accesses to infer a victim's activation or mitigation pattern. This is PRACLeak.

→ related card
Connection
18. How does the sampling-versus-counting split mirror heavy-hitter problems in streaming and databases?

Both ask for the elements that exceed a frequency threshold in a stream using bounded memory. Misra–Gries and Count-Min Sketch are the shared tools.

→ related card
19. How is GPUHammer's ML-weight attack related to the page-table-entry attack, conceptually?

Both flip a single high-leverage bit, a permission bit in one case and a model weight in the other. A tiny, targeted corruption therefore yields outsized control or damage.

→ related card
20. How does the threshold-setting problem resemble a moving target in any rate limiter?

A rate limiter tuned to a drifting threshold must either over-provision, which costs overhead, or risk misses. HC_first is that drifting threshold.

→ related card
Open
21. Should Rowhammer mitigation live in the DRAM, the controller, or the OS?

Unsettled. DRAM-only is deployable but has repeatedly failed. OS cooperation is flexible but needs new controller primitives. The likely answer is defence in depth across all three.

→ related card
22. How would you prove a DIMM Rowhammer-safe?

This is open. There are no accepted test vectors. You would need exhaustive characterisation across access patterns, temperature, aging, and per-chip variation, and that is currently infeasible.

→ related card
23. Is PRAC's roughly 10–18% overhead acceptable, and who decides?

It is contested. The answer depends on the workload and the threat model. Buyers ultimately pay in performance and power, and there is no neutral arbiter.

→ related card
24. Does per-chip variation make certification impossible in principle?

Possibly. If patterns don't transfer across chips, per-die certification may be the only sound approach, and that is economically brutal.

→ related card
