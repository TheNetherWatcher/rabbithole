Rowhammer on DDR5 — why in-DRAM mitigations keep failing

TL;DR. Hammering one DRAM row electrically disturbs its neighbors and flips bits you never touched. The defenses split into two camps: sampling (TRR) keeps a small list of "hot" rows and refreshes their neighbors — and is repeatedly broken because the list is bounded and its schedule can be learned (TRRespass → ZenHammer → Phoenix). Counting (DDR5's PRAC) keeps an exact per-row counter — the structural fix — but 2024–26 work shows it is expensive and itself a side channel.

Why it matters. A single flipped bit — a page-table permission, a pointer, an ML weight — can hand an attacker a machine they share with you. It's a software-triggered hardware bug, so you can't patch it away. Phoenix got root on a default DDR5 desktop in ~5 minutes (CVE-2025-6202).

0b. Plain English first

Explain it simply. RAM is a huge grid of tiny buckets, each holding a little water. Full = "1", empty = "0". The buckets are packed so tightly that sloshing one over and over makes a neighbor you never touched slowly leak — and if it drains past half before the automatic top-up truck comes around, its value silently flips. That is Rowhammer: you corrupt data you were never allowed to touch, just by reading your own data very fast, right next to it.

Why care: one flipped bit in the right place — a permission flag, a pointer, a security check — can hand an attacker control of a machine they share with you (a cloud server, your phone, a browser tab).

Everyday example. A library with shelves one millimetre apart. You yank one book off a shelf and slam it back, thousands of times a second. The vibration makes the book two rows over slip and fall — though you never touched that shelf. The librarian (refresh) re-shelves everything every 64 ms. Rowhammer is yanking fast enough that the neighbour falls before the librarian arrives. Every defense is a smarter librarian watching which books you yank — and attackers keep finding the moments the librarian isn't looking.

Bridge: now the same thing, technically.

1. Bare-minimum context

  • DRAM cell = 1 transistor + 1 capacitor. The bit is the capacitor's charge; charge leaks, so it must be refreshed. (Kim 2014)
  • Row buffer. A wordline turns on a whole row onto the bitlines; sense amps read and restore into the row buffer. Reading a row is destructive and immediately rewritten.
  • ACT / PRE / REF. Open a row, close it, top it up. Fastest same-bank reopen is tRC (~45–48 ns).
  • Refresh window tREFW = 64 ms (DDR3/4; 32 ms DDR5), split into REFs every tREFI (~7.8 µs DDR3/4, ~3.9 µs DDR5).
  • HC_first = hammer count to first flip. The number that governs everything — it falls ~10× per generation.
  • TRR / RFM / PRAC — the three in-DRAM defenses, in order. This dive is why the first two failed and whether the third is enough.

1b. Real-world context

What it is. A read-disturbance phenomenon: repeatedly activating one row leaks charge from physically adjacent rows fast enough to flip their bits before refresh. Kim et al. (ISCA 2014) found it in 110/129 DDR3 modules, with as few as 139K reads, in every module made 2012–2013. It's a scaling defect — worse as cells shrink — not a single-vendor bug.

Care about it if

  • You run multi-tenant hardware, a browser engine, or a mobile platform
  • You serve ML on shared GPUs
  • You design DRAM / memory controllers, or certify hardware

Mostly ignore if

  • Single-user, physically-secured, non-adversarial box
  • No untrusted code runs on the same DRAM as your secrets
  • (The attacker needs co-located execution)

Who should care: cloud/infra security, DRAM & memory-controller designers, browser/OS sandbox authors, ML-serving infra, hardware-security researchers.

■ Technical deep dive

2. The one-liner — and what it hides

"Rowhammer flips bits in memory you can't access by hammering an adjacent row; vendors added TRR to fix it."

What that hides:

  1. Why hammering disturbs a neighbor at all — three distinct physical effects, not "vibration".
  2. TRR is a sampling heuristic with bounded state — that bound is what every bypass exploits.
  3. The threshold keeps collapsing (139K → 4.8K → ~128), so today's cheap defense is tomorrow's ruin.
  4. Hammering isn't the only trigger — RowPress (holding a row open) and Half-Double (distance-2) break the "refresh the neighbor" assumption.
  5. DDR5's real fix is counting (PRAC), not sampling — and 2025–26 work shows even counting is expensive and leaky.

3. The problem

Software's bedrock assumption: memory you cannot name, you cannot change. DRAM scaling broke it. By ~20 nm, the charge separating "1" from "0" and the row spacing both shrank to where normal-rate activations leak a neighbor past its margin inside one 64 ms window.

The forcing constraint is the activation budget vs. HC_first. One bank admits ≈ tREFW/tRC = 64 ms / 47 ns ≈ 1.36M activations per window. At HC_first 139K you need ~10% of that aimed at one victim; at 4.8K it's trivial; projected chips sit near 128. The defense must catch an over-activated row before a threshold that's shrinking out from under it — cheaply, across billions of rows.

Minimum observed: 9.6K total (double-sided). Flips per fixed activation count rose ~500× over the span. (Revisiting RowHammer, 1580 chips)

4. Mental model

Leaky-bucket grid with a patrolling top-up truck. Each cell is a bucket slowly leaking; the refresh truck tops every bucket every 64 ms. Hammering shakes neighbors so they leak faster; flip = a neighbor empties past half before the truck arrives. TRR = a spotter on the truck noting which buckets you shake a lot, topping those neighbors early. PRAC = a counter on every bucket that rings a bell once it's been shaken too much.

Where it breaks: the disturbance isn't mechanical — it's three electrical effects, it can reach the bucket two rows away (Half-Double), and it can be driven by holding a bucket open (RowPress). A vibration intuition predicts none of these.

5. The mechanism, traced

Double-sided disturbance on a DDR4 bank. Victim V; aggressors A₁ = V−1, A₂ = V+1. Step through it:

The attacker never issues a single ACT V. After ~9.6K total activations (worst DDR4-era chip) V's charge crosses the sense-amp decision point and the next read latches the wrong value. Three physical effects do the damage (Kim 2014 §3): capacitive/EM coupling between wordlines, conductive bridges, and hot-carrier injection.

RowPress knob: keep A₁ open (long tAggON) instead of toggling — cumulative leakage replaces activation count. ~17.6× fewer at tAggON 7.8 µs, ~159× at 70.2 µs, a single activation at ~30 ms, worse when hot (RowPress, ISCA 2023).

6. The math

Refresh-window arithmetic

tREFI = tREFW / 8192 = 64 ms / 8192 = 7.8 µs · max ACT/window ≈ tREFW / tRC = 64 ms / 47 ns ≈ 1.36M

→ The attacker's whole budget is ~1.36M hammers; a defense only has to make "aim 4.8K of them at one victim undetected" impossible.

PARA — the stateless probabilistic defense

On every row close, with probability p refresh an adjacent row. Chance a victim is never rescued over N aggressor activations:

P_miss = (1 − p)^N (or (1 − p/2)^N if the coin picks one of two neighbors)

→ Each hammer is an independent coin flip for "refresh the neighbor now." Dodging rescue for N hammers needs every coin to come up tails — a probability that decays geometrically. With p ≈ 0.001–0.005, P_miss falls below the hardware hard-error budget (~10⁻¹⁵) at <0.75% cost. Zero state is the whole point — but the required p rises as HC_first falls.

Graphene — counting with Misra–Gries

To find every row exceeding ~T_RH/2 activations among N_ACT activations:

entries > N_ACT / (T_RH / 2) = 2 · N_ACT / T_RH

→ You don't need a counter per row — just one per "slot of hammering budget big enough to matter," a few hundred per bank. But it scales inversely with T_RH: as the threshold falls 1000 → 125, storage balloons ~207 KiB → ~1.5 MiB. (bound restated in CoMeT)

7. Why it works (and ablation)

The insight dividing the field: sampling vs. counting.

  • Sampling (TRR) keeps a small fixed set of hot candidates. Works only if real aggressors stay in that set. Remove the "few distinct rows" assumption → tracker overflows → an aggressor goes unrefreshed. That one property is what TRRespass, U-TRR, Blacksmith, ZenHammer, Phoenix all exploit.
  • Counting (PRAC) keeps an exact per-row count — nothing to overflow, nothing to under-sample. Remove exactness (shared/approx counters) and blind spots return.

Ablation: remove refresh → data dies in ms regardless. Remove neighbor-refresh-on-detection from TRR → it's plain refresh, flips trivially. Remove exact counting from PRAC → back to TRR's failure. Remove PRAC's Alert Back-Off window bound → attacker races activations in after the alert; the ~180 ns / ~3-ACT residual window is the controlled remnant of that risk.

Counting won because sampling was structurally, repeatedly broken. It hasn't ended the problem because exact per-row counting at DRAM scale is expensive — the whole 2024–26 PRAC-optimization literature exists to claw that cost back.

9. Where it breaks

TRR — bounded tracking. A tracker remembering N rows is beaten by N+1 aggressors.

  • TRRespass (S&P 2020): many-sided (~19 aggressors) overflowed trackers; 13/42 DDR4 DIMMs flipped (paper).
  • U-TRR (MICRO 2021): used retention failures to see exactly when/how many rows TRR refreshes; bypassed on 45 modules (arXiv).
  • Blacksmith (S&P 2022): non-uniform patterns flipped all 40/40 DDR4 DIMMs.

DDR5 TRR — Phoenix (S&P 2026, CVE-2025-6202). SK Hynix TRR samples over a 128-tREFI schedule (~8× longer than assumed); self-correcting synchronization lands hammers in its blind spots. 15/15 DIMMs flipped; root on a default DDR5 desktop in ~109 s; on-die ECC bypassed (ETH COMSEC).

The physics breaks "refresh the neighbor" too. Half-Double (Google 2021): disturbance reaches distance-2; TRR's own refresh of the intermediate row propagates it. RowPress (ISCA 2023): pressing, orthogonal to activation-count defenses.

Misconceptions (each wrong)

  • "ECC fixes it" → ECCploit crafts multi-flip words + correction-timing oracle.
  • "DDR5 is immune" → ZenHammer + Phoenix.
  • "Server/ECC memory is safe" → ECCploit targeted server ECC; ZenHammer hit AMD.
  • "It needs physical access" → Project Zero 2015 from userland; Rowhammer.js from JS.
  • "TRR solved it" → every bypass above.

10. In the wild

  • Exploits: Project Zero 2015 (PTE flip → kernel privesc); Drammer (Android); Rowhammer.js (browser); Throwhammer (RDMA/network).
  • GPUHammer (USENIX Sec 2025): GDDR6 on RTX A6000 (non-ECC default); one weight-bit flip dropped accuracy ~80%→0.1%; 56–80% across 5 nets (arXiv). ECC GPUs (H100, RTX 5090) detect these.
  • Deployed today (2025–26): TRR + on-die ECC + RFM — all bypassable on default DDR5 (Phoenix). Google: PRAC is not in the DDR5 systems it evaluated — standardized for future revisions, not shipping.
  • 2015 stopgap: Apple/HP/Cisco/Lenovo/IBM BIOS forced 2× refresh (tREFI 7.8→3.9 µs). Partial, costly.

11. Frontier (2024–2026)

Two live fronts: (a) make PRAC practical & secure, (b) attack PRAC itself.

Phoenix (ETH COMSEC + Google, S&P 2026) — CVE-2025-6202

Reverse-engineered SK Hynix TRR's 128-tREFI sampling period, found lightly-sampled sub-intervals, self-synchronized hammers into them and re-aligned after missed refreshes. First end-to-end DDR5 exploit on default settings; root in ~5 min; on-die ECC bypassed. Lesson: sampling is structurally defeatable once the schedule is known — the capstone case for counting. (PDF · code)

QPRAC (HPCA 2025) — secure and practical PRAC

MOAT (ASPLOS 2025) showed naive PRAC queues are "tardy" — counts reach ~1150 vs. a threshold of 128 before mitigation lands. QPRAC adds an N-entry priority service queue per bank tracking the top-N rows, with provable bounds; claims security down to T_RH = 71. Still pays per-activation counter cost. (arXiv)

PRACLeak (ISCA 2025) — the defense is a new side channel

PRAC's ABO/RFM mitigation actions are observable via memory-access latency — a timing channel that leaks secrets, plus forced-ABO DoS. Follow-ups: AutoPRAC (model-checking to auto-find PRAC attacks); a CCS 2025 result that both sampling- and counting-based defenses leak via mitigation activity. (arXiv)

More: MOAT · RowPress IEEE Micro 2024 · combined RH+RowPress · CoMeT (DSN 2024) · SpyHammer · Fundamentally Understanding (ASP-DAC 2023). (Exact venues/dates for GPUThor, DejaVu, ScaleDisturb, PVAC, Loaded Dice, ARTA, AutoPRAC, FP-Rowhammer, SpyHammer flagged unverified — inferred from arXiv IDs.)

11b. Open problems

  • Is PRAC enough, at what cost? Closes sampling holes, but precharge-time read-modify-write inflates timings (~10–18% reported); the spec "leaves some questions unanswered" and flips still seen on PRAC paths (NDSS 2026). Major advance, not closed.
  • Threshold-setting. Every counter scheme needs a threshold below HC_first — but HC_first keeps falling (139K → 4.8K → ~128). No stable safe margin.
  • Counting at scale. Exact per-row counters over billions of rows are costly; where to store them is unsettled.
  • Where should mitigation live? DRAM-only (deployable, failed), controller, or OS-cooperative (SAFARI's Self-Managing DRAM vs. VUSec/MSR). No consensus.
  • Verification. No accepted method to prove a DIMM safe; TRR was unauditable; "RowHammer-free" labels proved false; aging uncharacterized.
  • Supply-chain variation. Vulnerability varies by vendor/die/batch, even within one chip — patterns don't transfer, defeating one-size thresholds.
  • Economic misalignment. TRR was optional in JEDEC, an undocumented black box; VUSec argues self-interested vendors under-invest while users bear the cost.

13. Hands-on (≤30 min, non-destructive)

  1. Watch RAS/ECC counters (read-only): rasdaemon → ras-mc-ctl --summary / --error-count → edac-util -v. A rising CE count on one DIMM is the fingerprint of marginal cells.
  2. Compute your activation budget. Budget ≈ tREFW/tRC; divide by a plausible HC_first. Watch it balloon as you plug in the projected HC_first of 128. (Use the slider in §6.)
  3. Reason through PARA. p=0.002, N=50,000 → P_miss ≈ e⁻⁵⁰ ≈ 2×10⁻²². Now set N (the hammers you must survive) to match HC_first=128 and ask what p you'd need — and what it costs.
  4. (Your hardware only) Memtest86 test 13 runs a Rowhammer check from a bootable stick.

14. Question ladder

Mechanism

What are the three physical effects by which activating a row disturbs a neighbor?
Capacitive/EM coupling between adjacent wordlines (partially turns on neighbor transistors), conductive bridges/shorts, and hot-carrier injection altering transistor leakiness over time.
Why is reading a DRAM row destructive, and what restores it?
Sensing dumps the capacitor's charge onto the bitline to detect it, draining the cell; the sense amplifier simultaneously rewrites the charge back ("restore").
Why is double-sided hammering worse than single-sided?
Coupling from both bracketing aggressors (V−1 and V+1) adds, so the victim loses charge from two sides — fewer total activations to cross the margin.
How does RowPress differ physically from classic hammering?
It keeps the aggressor open (long tAggON) rather than toggling; cumulative leakage while open replaces many ACTs — 1–2 orders of magnitude fewer, worse when hot.
What makes Half-Double reach a distance-2 row?
Coupling isn't strictly nearest-neighbor; heavy access to a distance-2 row plus a little to the intermediate row propagates disturbance, and TRR's refresh of the intermediate row acts as an extra access.

Why

Why does HC_first fall with each process node?
Smaller cells hold less charge (smaller noise margin) and sit at tighter pitch (stronger coupling), so less injected disturbance flips a bit.
Why did the field move from sampling (TRR) to counting (PRAC)?
Sampling keeps bounded state and can be overflowed or dodged by learning the schedule; counting is ground truth with nothing to overflow.
Why is PARA stateless, and why is that both strength and weakness?
It stores nothing — just a per-close coin flip; strength = zero area/state; weakness = only a statistical bound, and required p rises as HC_first falls, raising overhead.
Why does Graphene need only ~2·N_ACT/T_RH counters?
You only catch rows exceeding ~T_RH/2 within N_ACT total; Misra–Gries guarantees that with counters proportional to stream/threshold, far fewer than one per row.
Why doesn't on-die ECC stop Rowhammer — and why can it make detection harder?
It's a reliability code, not adversarial; multi-flip words defeat SECDED, and by silently correcting isolated flips it hides the onset of corruption from monitoring.

Counterfactual

If HC_first stabilized at 50K forever, which defenses would suddenly be "good enough"?
Increased-refresh and PARA with modest p would be cheap and sufficient; the crisis is driven by HC_first collapsing.
Remove the ABO back-off window bound from PRAC — what reopens?
The attacker races a burst of activations in after the alert but before mitigation; the bounded ~180 ns/~3-ACT window exists to cap exactly that.
If trackers had unlimited entries, would TRR be secure? At what cost?
Yes in principle, but one entry per row is billions of counters — the area/latency/energy is precisely why counting was avoided until PRAC's optimized forms.

Adversarial

How does Phoenix stay synchronized with a 128-tREFI sampling schedule?
It reverse-engineered the period and uses self-correcting synchronization to re-align hammers into lightly-sampled sub-intervals after any missed refresh.
Why does many-sided hammering defeat a bounded tracker?
The tracker holds N candidates; N+1 distinct aggressors guarantee at least one real aggressor is never selected for neighbor-refresh.
How does ECCploit turn error correction into an oracle?
A corrected single-bit read takes measurably longer; timing it reveals which flips the code absorbed, guiding the attacker toward an undetectable multi-flip word.
How could a mitigation mechanism (PRAC) itself leak secrets?
Its mitigation actions (ABO/RFM stalls) change memory latency observably; an attacker times their own accesses to infer a victim's activation/mitigation pattern (PRACLeak).

Connection

How does sampling-vs-counting mirror heavy-hitter problems in streaming/databases?
Both are "find elements exceeding a frequency threshold in a stream with bounded memory" — Misra–Gries / Count-Min-Sketch are the shared tools.
How is GPUHammer's ML-weight attack related to the PTE attack conceptually?
Both flip a single high-leverage bit — a permission bit vs. a model weight — so a tiny targeted corruption yields outsized control/damage.
How does threshold-setting resemble a moving target in any rate-limiter?
A rate-limiter tuned to a drifting threshold must over-provision (overhead) or risk misses; HC_first is that drifting threshold.

Open

Should mitigation live in DRAM, the controller, or the OS?
Unsettled: DRAM-only is deployable but has failed; OS-cooperative is flexible but needs new controller primitives; likely defense-in-depth across all three.
How would you prove a DIMM Rowhammer-safe?
Open: no accepted test vectors; you'd need exhaustive characterization across patterns, temperature, aging, and per-chip variation — currently infeasible.
Is PRAC's ~10–18% overhead acceptable, and who decides?
Contested: depends on workload/threat model; buyers ultimately pay in performance/power, with no neutral arbiter.
Does per-chip variation make certification impossible in principle?
Possibly: if patterns don't transfer across chips, per-die certification may be the only sound approach — economically brutal.
