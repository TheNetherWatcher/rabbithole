Linux EEVDF scheduler: virtual lag, eligibility, virtual deadlines

Linux 6.6 replaced CFS's "smallest vruntime" rule and its pile of latency heuristics with two checks. A task is eligible only if it is not ahead of the weighted average, and among eligible tasks the one with the earliest virtual deadline runs. Each request's size sets its deadline, so latency is chosen per task while long-run share is still set by weight.

Why it matters: every mainline kernel from 6.6 on runs it, and it is the source of most latency tuning questions and kernel-upgrade regressions in CPU-bound services.

The whole idea in 9 scrolls

How Linux 6.6 lets short turns jump the line, without extra CPU

One CPU, three tasks. A and B each ask for 3 ms turns. C asks for 1 ms. The dots show how much each task has been served, and V is their weighted average. Right now all three sit on V.

The old rule. CFS ran whichever task had been served least. That is fair over time. But a task wanting 100 µs turns and one wanting 10 ms turns got the same treatment, so latency had to come from extra tuning.

Eligible means not ahead. C ran for 1 ms, so its dot moves past V. A task ahead of the average is not eligible and cannot run. A and B are behind, so they are.

Pick the earliest deadline. Each request gets a deadline: its position plus slice ÷ weight. At t = 3 ms everyone is square again, C has deadline 2 and A and B have 3. Among the eligible tasks, the earliest deadline runs, so C goes.

Working. The tasks take turns one millisecond at a time. Each turn puts the task out of eligibility until the average catches up. The first 12 ms of the trace are on the diagram.

Over 24 ms, each gets 8 ms. A, B and C each get 8 ms of CPU. The short slice changed when C ran, not how much it got. Short slices buy low latency, not extra share.

Weight sets the share. With equal slices, weight 2048 against 1024 gives HLHHLHHLHHLH: 8 ms against 4, a 2:1 ratio over 12 ms.

The cost. Every change of task is a context switch, and short turns mean more of them. The share stays the same. Slice trades latency for switch rate, and weight trades share.

The numbers. The default base slice runs from 0.7 to 2.8 ms, and EEVDF arrived in Linux 6.6. Each card below takes one piece apart. Guess first where a card asks you to.

Plain English
In everyday words

A fair barista who lets short turns jump the line

Everyone gets an equal share of the barista's day. EEVDF keeps a ledger of who is owed time, and gives each customer a finish-by time that is earlier for shorter turns.

Short turns are served sooner, while total time over the day stays equal.

Explain it simply. Picture one barista (the CPU) and a line of customers (programs). Everyone should get a fair share of the barista's time. The old Linux rule (CFS) was: "serve whoever has been served the least so far." Fair, but it had no way to say "this customer only wants a 20-second chat, serve them quickly." Linux bolted on a pile of guesses (heuristics) about who "seems interactive."

EEVDF replaces the guesses with two honest ideas. Fairness: keep a running ledger of who is owed time and who has had too much. Only people who are owed time (or square) are allowed to be served next. Urgency: every customer states how long a turn they want. Short-turn customers get an earlier "finish-by" time. Among everyone allowed, serve whoever's finish-by time comes first. Short turn = served sooner, but total time over the day is still equal. Latency and fairness become two separate dials.

Everyday example

Three people share a whiteboard: Ann and Bob each want 3-minute turns, Cy wants 1-minute turns. All three are equally "owed" at the start. Cy's finish-by is earliest (1 min vs 3), so Cy goes first for 1 minute. Now Cy has had more than average, so Cy is not allowed until the others catch up. Ann goes (her finish-by ties with Bob's), then Bob. When everybody is square again, Cy's next finish-by is again the earliest, so Cy jumps in. After a full day each has had exactly the same total minutes, but Cy got many more, shorter turns, so Cy never waited long.

The technical version follows in the next cards: the ledger is lag, the finish-by time is the virtual deadline, and "allowed" is eligible.

Context
§1 · Prerequisites

Six things to know first

  • Runqueue: the per-CPU list of runnable tasks.
  • Weight: nice 0 is 1024; each nice step is about 1.25×.
  • vruntime: CPU time scaled by 1024/w, so heavy tasks age slower.
  • Red-black tree of runnable entities; cgroups nest them.
  • A CPU scheduler picks which runnable task runs next on each core's runqueue (per-CPU; the load balancer separately moves tasks between runqueues).
  • Weight w comes from nice value (nice 0 → 1024; each nice step ≈ 1.25×).
  • vruntime v = CPU time consumed scaled by 1024/w, so heavy tasks age slower.
  • Proportional share / GPS: the ideal fluid scheduler gives each task w_i/Σw of the CPU at every instant. Real schedulers approximate it with discrete slices.
  • Linux keeps runnable entities in a red-black tree; sched_entity is the schedulable unit (a task or a cgroup group).
  • cgroups nest entities hierarchically; each level has its own runqueue.
§1b · Real-world context

Every mainline kernel since 6.6 runs it, mostly unseen

Default on Ubuntu 24.04, Debian 13, Fedora and Arch, on Android GKI 6.6+, and in containers through cpu.weight. Knobs: nice, a per-thread custom slice (6.12+), and sched_ext for whole custom policies.

What it is

EEVDF is a proportional-share scheduling algorithm from Stoica & Abdel-Wahab (1995). Linux 6.6 (Oct 2023) replaced CFS's "pick smallest vruntime" logic with it. Each task has a weight (how much CPU it deserves) and a request size/slice (how long a turn it wants). The scheduler runs the eligible task (not ahead of its fair share) with the earliest virtual deadline.

Why it exists

CFS equalised vruntime but had no notion of latency, so Linux accreted heuristics: sched_latency_ns, sched_min_granularity_ns, sched_wakeup_granularity_ns, GENTLE_FAIR_SLEEPERS, sleeper bonuses. They conflicted and were hard to reason about. Corbet's LWN piece reports EEVDF let Zijlstra delete ~450 lines of such code (a secondary claim, not re-counted here). Latency-sensitive workloads (audio, UI, RPC servers) needed a first-class way to ask for short turns without raising weight (which would steal CPU share).

How it's used

Most users touch nothing. The knobs:

// per-task custom slice (Linux >= 6.12): ask for 0.5 ms turns, normal weight
struct sched_attr a = {
    .size = sizeof(a),
    .sched_policy = SCHED_OTHER,
    .sched_nice = 0,
    .sched_runtime = 500 * 1000,   // ns; kernel clamps to [100us, 100ms]
};
syscall(SYS_sched_setattr, 0, &a, 0);   // 0 = calling thread
nice -n -5 ./app                           # weight, i.e. CPU share
cat /sys/kernel/debug/sched/base_slice_ns  # default slice (needs debugfs)
cat /sys/kernel/debug/sched/features       # PLACE_LAG RUN_TO_PARITY DELAY_DEQUEUE ...
echo NO_RUN_TO_PARITY > /sys/kernel/debug/sched/features   # A/B test a feature

Default base slice (verified in fair.c): 700000 ns × (1 + ilog2(min(ncpu, 8))) = 0.7 / 1.4 / 2.1 / 2.8 ms for 1 / 2 / 4 / ≥8 CPUs. Distro docs often say "3 ms" (an older constant of 0.75 ms × 4).

When to use it, and when not

  • Use the fair class plus a custom slice for latency-sensitive but non-RT work: UI threads, RPC workers, audio mixers that aren't hard real-time.
  • Skip for hard deadlines. Use SCHED_DEADLINE (admission-controlled CBS/EDF) or SCHED_FIFO/RR. EEVDF virtual deadlines give uniform allocation, not timeliness guarantees against arbitrary load.
  • Skip for workload-specific policy (database background-task isolation, game frame pacing). Use sched_ext.
  • Signs you picked wrong: p99 latency tracks nr_running even with tiny slices (you need RT or isolation), or short-slice threads thrash caches with context switches.

Who should care: backend and infra engineers chasing tail latency; SREs reading regressions after a kernel upgrade (6.5 → 6.6); audio and game developers; kernel and BPF-scheduler authors; anyone running tight cgroup CPU limits.

Technical deep dive
§2 · The one-liner

"Earliest virtual deadline among the eligible" hides five things

The slogan hides what the "virtual" clock is, how eligibility is tested, what sleep does to lag, that slice and weight are orthogonal, and that the decision covers one runqueue only.

"EEVDF runs the eligible task with the earliest virtual deadline."

That sentence hides five things:

  1. "Virtual": what clock? V is a weighted average of all vruntimes, and it moves when tasks join, leave, or get reweighted.
  2. "Eligible": lag ≥ 0, tested without division in fixed point, with overflow handling. What if lag is large, or a task sleeps?
  3. Sleep: a task that sleeps leaves the average. If its lag is forgotten at sleep, tasks can game it; if it is remembered, placement must compensate for the task's own effect on V.
  4. Slice vs weight: slice sets when, weight sets how much. They are orthogonal.
  5. One CPU only: EEVDF decides within one runqueue. Cross-CPU fairness is the (unchanged) load balancer's job.
§3 · The problem

CFS let sleepers jump the queue, and each tuning knob broke another

CFS placed a waking task near the minimum vruntime, so it won at once. Latency came from tunables in mixed units that fought each other. Needed: a bounded fairness error, a latency request independent of weight, and fewer magic numbers.

6.6the first mainline kernel to swap CFS's pick rule for EEVDF (Oct 2023).

CFS picks the task with the smallest vruntime. That gives long-run fairness, but a task that wakes after sleeping is placed near min_vruntime and wins immediately. Placement was the only latency lever, controlled by tunables with mixed units (ns, scaled by log2 of the CPU count). A task that wants 100 µs turns and a task that wants 10 ms turns got the same treatment unless priority was abused.

Lag was unbounded in theory for unfairly placed sleepers, and was not even a tracked quantity. Server regressions and desktop stutter were each fixed by retuning one knob and breaking another.

What was needed:

  • (a) a bounded-error fairness guarantee,
  • (b) a per-task latency request orthogonal to weight,
  • (c) fewer magic numbers.
§4 · Mental model

A bank ledger with a stopwatch: lag is the balance, deadline the due date

Lag is your balance with the CPU, and positive means it owes you. Only non-negative accounts get served. The catch: the reference point is a weighted mean that moves whenever tasks join, leave or reweight.

Bank ledger plus stopwatch. Lag is your account balance with the CPU. Positive means the CPU owes you time; negative means you overdrew. Only non-negative accounts may be served (eligibility). Each request carries a due date (its virtual deadline), and the scheduler serves the earliest due date among the eligible.

Where the analogy breaks. The "bank" is a relative ledger. V (the reference) is the weighted mean of everyone currently queued, so balances change when others arrive or leave, without you doing anything. That is why sleeping and waking, reweighting, and cgroups produce most of the real bugs.
§5 · Mechanism, traced

Eligibility and deadlines pick every millisecond

Guess first Three equal-weight tasks run for 24 ms. A and B ask for 3 ms turns, C asks for 1 ms. How much CPU does C get?

8 ms, the same as A and B. The eligibility check forces everyone back to the average, so the short slice changes when C runs, not how much it gets.

Three equal-weight tasks ask for 3 ms, 3 ms and 1 ms slices. Over 24 ms each gets 8 ms, the same as the others. The eligibility check and the deadline order decide every millisecond.

What to see:

  • Equal CPU (8 / 8 / 8) because eligibility forces everyone back to V.
  • C asks for shorter turns, so its deadline ve + 1 is always earlier than ve + 3. Over 24 ms it still gets 8 ms, the same as the others. A short slice buys low latency, not extra share.
  • At t = 7 → 8 B finishes its 3 ms request (v = 3 ≥ vd = 3) and gets vd = 3 + 3 = 6 (update_deadline()).
  • Weights: H (w = 2048) vs L (w = 1024) with equal slices gives HLHHLHHLHHLH: H 8, L 4, a 2:1 ratio. H's vruntime advances at half speed, so it stays eligible longer.

Kernel flow for the same events:

Real code (kernel/sched/fair.c, current master, fetched 2026-10-04):

static struct sched_entity *pick_eevdf(struct cfs_rq *cfs_rq, bool protect)
{
	...
	/* Pick the leftmost entity if it's eligible */
	if (se && entity_eligible(cfs_rq, se)) { best = se; goto found; }
	/* Heap search for the EEVD entity */
	while (node) {
		struct rb_node *left = node->rb_left;
		/* Eligible entities in left subtree are always better choices, since they have earlier deadlines. */
		if (left && vruntime_eligible(cfs_rq, __node_2_se(left)->min_vruntime)) { node = left; continue; }
		se = __node_2_se(node);
		if (entity_eligible(cfs_rq, se)) { best = se; break; }
		node = node->rb_right;
	}
found:
	if (!best || (curr && entity_before(curr, best))) best = curr;
	return best;
}

The tree key is the deadline. Each node stores the min_vruntime of its subtree, so whole subtrees with no eligible task are pruned. Pick is O(log n).

Sleep and wake

The subtle part. On dequeue, update_entity_lag() stores vlag = V − v, clamped. On enqueue, place_entity() restores it:

vi = V − vlagi′,   where vlagi′ = vlagi · (W + wi) / W→ in plain terms: remember how far behind you were, then inflate it, because adding yourself moves the average.
deadline = vi + slice · 1024 / wi→ new tasks get half a slice instead (PLACE_DEADLINE_INITIAL).

The inflation exists because adding the entity changes V itself. The derivation is in the next card.

DELAY_DEQUEUE (6.12): a task going to sleep with negative lag stays on the tree as sched_delayed, ineligible, burning off its lag in virtual time. It is only really dequeued when it is picked (then its lag is ≥ 0 by definition) or woken. DELAY_ZERO clips lag to ≤ 0 on a delayed dequeue.

§6 · The math

Lag stays within one request of the ideal share

The bound is −c < lag < max(cmax, q), where c is the request size and q the quantum. Lags sum to zero because V is the weighted mean. A joining task's saved lag is inflated by (W+w)/W.

Setup (paper)

Tasks i have weights w_i. Virtual time advances at a rate inversely proportional to the total weight:

V(t) = ∫0t dτ / Σj wj(τ)→ in plain terms: V is "how much service one unit of weight should have received so far". A task with weight w ideally got w · ΔV of CPU.

Lag. lagi(t) = Si(t₀, t) − si(t₀, t): ideal minus actual service. Positive means owed.

Kernel form

The kernel tracks virtual lag, which avoids carrying a w_i everywhere:

lagi = wi (V − vi),   vlagi = V − vi,   V = Σ wj vj / Σ wj→ in plain terms: V is the weighted mean vruntime. Being left of the mean means being owed.

Eligibility

lagi ≥ 0 ⇔ V ≥ vi ⇔ Σ wj (vj − v0) ≥ (vi − v0) Σ wj→ the kernel uses the right-hand side, cross-multiplied, because division loses precision (the comment in fair.c says "using avg_vruntime() > se->vruntime is inaccurate").

It also keeps sum_w_vruntime = Σ (v_j − v_0) w_j relative to a moving zero_vruntime, so the sums don't overflow, and it uses __int128 for the product.

Virtual eligible time and deadline (paper)

V(e) = V(t₀) + s(t₀, t) / w    V(d) = V(e) + c / w→ c is the request size. In the kernel, vd = ve + r/w with ve = v and r the slice in ns, scaled by calc_delta_fair. A request of size c at weight w "costs" c/w virtual time, so heavy tasks have short virtual deadlines per real millisecond and run more often.

Placement derivation (from the kernel comment)

Add entity i with virtual lag vl_i and weight w_i to a queue with total weight W and mean V:

V′ = (W·V + wi(V − vli)) / (W + wi) = V − wi·vli / (W + wi)
vl′i = V′ − vi = vli · W / (W + wi)→ the lag after joining is strictly smaller than before: the newcomer drags the mean toward itself. To preserve lag, inflate it: vli = vl′i · (W + wi) / W. This is the lag *= load + weight; lag /= load step in place_entity(). A creditor who walks in changes the average, so you must pre-compensate or their credit evaporates.

Lag conservation and bounds (paper)

Σi lagi(t) = 0 always, because everyone's ledger sums to zero when V is the weighted mean. The theorem from Stoica's 1995 slides: with request size c for task k and quantum q:

−c < lagk(t) < max(cmax, q)→ a request completes no later than d + q after its virtual deadline. The proof sketch splits tasks by whether their deadline is before or after d. Since lags sum to zero and the "after" group can't be owed more than q, every request in the "before" group finishes by d + q. In plain terms: nobody is ever more than about one maximum slice ahead of or behind the ideal fluid schedule, which is the best you can do for a quantised scheduler.

Why not something simpler? WFQ-style "smallest virtual finish time" with no eligibility test can serve a task ahead of its fluid schedule (the observation behind WF2Q). The eligibility gate is what gives the lower lag bound.

§7 · Why it works

Eligibility bounds unfairness; deadlines let each task choose its latency

Remove eligibility and tiny-slice tasks starve the rest. Remove deadlines and you get CFS, fair with no latency control. Remove lag memory and one sleep wipes out a debt. Each piece covers a different failure.

Two properties factor the problem:

  • Eligibility ⇒ bounded unfairness. A task ahead of V cannot be picked. Time is only handed to tasks at or behind the mean.
  • Deadline order among eligibles ⇒ latency chosen by the task. A short r_i gives an earlier vd_i, so the task is picked first among the eligible.

Ablations

  • Remove eligibility: pure EDF on virtual deadlines. A stream of tiny-slice tasks starves the rest, and lag is unbounded.
  • Remove deadlines (pick minimum vruntime): that is CFS. Fair, but with no latency control.
  • Remove lag memory (PLACE_LAG off): a sleep resets debt, so a task can sleep briefly each slice and wipe out its negative lag.
  • Remove DELAY_DEQUEUE: the same exploit comes back through ineligible-then-sleep. Or lag is preserved forever, which penalises long sleepers.
  • Remove RUN_TO_PARITY: every wakeup with a slightly earlier deadline preempts, so context switches explode (the hackbench-style regressions after the merge).
§8 · Knobs & alternatives

Slice sets when a task runs, weight sets how much

Guess first A thread asks for a custom slice of 1 µs. What range does the kernel clamp it into?

100 µs to 100 ms. The kernel clamps sched_runtime into that range (Linux 6.12 and later). Shorter requests are raised to 100 µs.

Slice and weight are orthogonal. Custom slices run from 100 µs to 100 ms. Alternatives are CFS-style heuristics, SCHED_DEADLINE reservations and BPF sched_ext.

EEVDF won mainline because it has one theory-backed rule and removes tunables.

§9 · Where it breaks

A 2024 overflow turned a weight change into a NULL dereference

Guess first Which kernel release fixed the CVE-2024-35985 overflow?

6.9, fixed in 6.9-rc6 (commit 1560d1f6eb6b) and backported. An s64 multiply overflowed, so every task looked ineligible, and pick_eevdf() returned NULL.

CVE-2024-35985 made every entity look ineligible, so the picker returned NULL. Tick granularity, per-CPU-only fairness and cgroup reweighting also bite, and "short slice = more CPU" is a myth.

  • Regressions at merge (6.6): over-scheduling when lag ≈ slice (fixed by "curb wakeup preemption" and RUN_TO_PARITY, per LKML and LWN reporting). vhost/netperf and schbench/tbench shifts were attributed to lag-based placement. These are secondary reports, and the magnitudes vary by hardware (unverified).
  • CVE-2024-35985 (verified, Debian tracker): reweight_eevdf() scaled vlag without the clamp used elsewhere. An s64 multiply overflowed, entity_eligible() returned false for everything, and pick_eevdf() returned NULL, which led to a NULL dereference. "Rare, but fatal when it does happen." Fixed in 6.9-rc6 (commit 1560d1f6eb6b) and backported. The overflow-guarded vruntime_eligible() (check_mul_overflow, __int128) in current source is the same class of defence.
  • Per-CPU only: no cross-CPU fairness. Bursty tasks pile onto one CPU until the load balancer reacts, which takes tens to 100+ ms.
  • cgroup hierarchies: reweighting a group entity changes the vruntime units of its queued members, and lag clamping and deadline pushes interact. Entity-placement fixes were still landing around 6.13 (a January 2025 last-minute fix, per Phoronix) and later. The exact later commit history is unverified: research notes had inconsistent dates.
  • Tick granularity: accounting is tick-based. With a 3 ms slice and HZ=1000, a task can hit its deadline up to 1 ms early or late. hrtick exists but is off by default (the HRTICK feature is false in the file fetched).
  • Misconception: "short slice = more CPU." No. Same weight means the same long-run share. You get it in smaller, earlier pieces, and you pay the switch cost.
  • Misconception: "EEVDF is real-time." Virtual deadlines are not wall-clock deadlines. The bound is d + q in virtual terms on one runqueue.
  • Heterogeneous cores: EEVDF itself is capacity-blind. P/E-core and energy-aware decisions live in wakeup placement and load balancing.
§10 · In the wild

Linux adds clamped lag, parity protection and delayed dequeue to the paper

RUN_TO_PARITY protects the running task. Delayed dequeue (6.12) stops sleep-based lag gaming. Cgroups each get their own EEVDF, and an augmented tree prunes subtrees with no eligible task. The 1995 paper has none of these.

Linux deviates from the 1995 paper in several ways:

  • Clock-driven, not exact quanta. The deadline is updated in update_deadline() once v ≥ vd. The source comment calls the update "good enough" next to the strict vd_i += N*r_i/w_i.
  • Lag is clamped on dequeue and remembered across sleep. The paper assumes tasks leave and arrive with zero or positive lag.
  • RUN_TO_PARITY (not in the paper) protects the current task, so wakeups don't thrash.
  • Slice protection with the shortest slice in the queue (cfs_rq_min_slice) while running to parity.
  • Hierarchical. Each cgroup has its own cfs_rq. Group entities take part in their parent's EEVDF with h_load weights.
  • Augmented red-black tree. The key is the deadline; the augmentation is the subtree min_vruntime (and min_slice), used for pruning.
  • Delayed dequeue (6.12) is a Linux-specific answer to sleep-based lag gaming. The paper has none.
  • The kernel docs say: "There are ongoing discussions on how to manage lag, especially for sleeping tasks."

§11 · Frontier

Policy moved into BPF and LLM-written schedulers

Once fairness and latency were settled per runqueue, the work moved to policy (sched_ext, synthesised schedulers), locality hints and proxy execution for priority inversion. SchedCP claims 1.79× on a kernel build, unreplicated.

All arXiv entries were confirmed to exist through the arXiv API on 2026-10-04. Titles and dates below come from it. Research-agent summaries about venues and speedups are not independently verified unless stated.

  • Proxy Execution (Stultz et al., Google) generalises priority inheritance. A mutex-blocked task stays queued, and the lock owner runs on its behalf. The Kconfig option SCHED_PROXY_EXEC exists in master, depends on EXPERT and !SCHED_CLASS_EXT. It is merged and experimental, and not yet combined with sched_ext (verified in init/Kconfig).
  • sched_ext (merged in Linux 6.12) is a BPF-defined scheduler class. Production examples include Valve/Igalia's scx_lavd (latency-criticality-aware virtual deadline), with reported fleet use at Meta per Phoronix and LPC 2025 (secondary). Docs: docs.kernel.org/scheduler/sched-ext.
  • SchedCP, "Towards Agentic OS: An LLM Agent Framework for Linux Schedulers" (arXiv 2509.01245, Sep 2025). An MCP-server control plane where LLM agents synthesise and deploy sched_ext policies. It claims up to 1.79× on a kernel build. That is an abstract-level claim and unreplicated. The figure below shows the authors' own benchmark.
  • Mixture-of-Schedulers (arXiv 2511.11628, Nov 2025): a learned router over expert scheduling policies.
  • UFS, "Unfair by design" (arXiv 2605.02377, May 2026): an eBPF sched_ext scheduler for mixed database workloads. It restricts background tasks to idle CPUs, preempts instantly, and uses eBPF-map hints against priority inversion. Its motivating claim is that OS priorities don't isolate.
  • Affinity Tailor (arXiv 2604.27915, Apr 2026): userspace-guided kernel scheduling at Google. It uses compact CPU sets as affinity hints rather than hard partitions, to recover locality (cache and chiplet LLC). Headline numbers in the research notes (+12% / +7%) are not verified; check the paper.
  • Latency-Aware Group Scheduling (arXiv 2508.15703, Aug 2025): reduces context switching in densely packed clusters.
  • AKTS (arXiv 2609.12276, Sep 2026): sub-microsecond kernel policy switching for LM agents. The title and date are verified; the content is unread.
  • Ekiben (arXiv 2306.15076, EuroSys 2024 per research notes): a safe Rust scheduler framework with live upgrade. It is older, but it is the basis of the "scheduler development velocity" line.

Left: SchedCP's split between an AI agent layer (what to optimise) and a safe control plane (how to observe and act, with eBPF loaded through a verifier). Right: Ekiben's design, with a user-space manager that upgrades a live scheduler module.

Common thread: once EEVDF had fixed the mechanism for per-runqueue fairness and latency, active work moved to policy (BPF or LLM-synthesised schedulers), to locality (affinity hints), and to priority inversion (proxy execution).

§11b · Open problems

Latency SLOs, global fairness and heterogeneous cores are still open

Slices are the only latency handle, and per-runqueue bounds don't compose into a global one under a periodic balancer. Cgroup reweighting and asymmetric cores are unsolved. LLM-written BPF schedulers are checked for memory safety, not fairness.

  1. Expressing latency SLOs. Slice is only a proxy. "p99 ≤ 5 ms" cannot be expressed without changing weight or relying on RT. The custom slice (6.12) is the first step.
  2. Cross-CPU fairness. EEVDF's theorem is per runqueue. Global share depends on a periodic load balancer that predates it. There is no global lag.
  3. Cgroup hierarchy correctness. Reweighting nested group entities moves vruntime units. Clamping and reweight cascades have produced bugs; CVE-2024-35985 was one.
  4. Heterogeneous, SMT and NUMA. Capacity, cache and sibling-thread effects are not in the lag model.
  5. Tick-free precision. Deadline-hit detection is quantised by the tick unless hrtick is used, and hrtick is costly.
  6. Interactive tuning. Gaming and desktop communities still ship BORE or tuned slice defaults (CachyOS). That suggests the default slice (0.7–2.8 ms) and parity protection don't fit every interactive profile (anecdotal).
  7. Policy synthesis safety. For LLM-written BPF schedulers, the verifier proves memory safety, not fairness or starvation-freedom.
§12 · Lineage

From GPS (1993) through CFS (2007) to EEVDF in Linux (2023)

GPS (1993) → WFQ and WF2Q → stride scheduling and the EEVDF paper (1995) → CFS (Linux 2.6.23, 2007) → EEVDF in Linux 6.6 (2023) → custom slices and sched_ext (6.12).

GPS (Parekh–Gallager, 1993) → WFQ/PGPS (Demers–Keshav–Shenker, 1989) → WF2Q (Bennett–Zhang, 1996). Stride scheduling (Waldspurger, 1995) → EEVDF (Stoica–Abdel-Wahab, 1995; revisited in RTSS 2000) → CFS (Molnár, 2007, Linux 2.6.23) → EEVDF in Linux (Zijlstra, 6.6, 2023) → DELAY_DEQUEUE and custom slices (6.12) → sched_ext (6.12) → proxy execution (experimental).

§14 · Question ladder

Test yourself

22 questions from mechanism to open research. Reveal, then mark ✓ or ✗; marks feed your progress ring.

Answer in your head first, then reveal and mark yourself honestly. Marks are saved in this browser and feed the progress ring.

Mechanism
1. Walk pick_eevdf() when the leftmost-by-deadline node is ineligible. Which subtree does it descend?

Loop: go to the left child while the left subtree's min_vruntime is eligible, because an eligible task with an earlier deadline exists there. Otherwise test the current node, and otherwise go right. The first eligible node found is the earliest-deadline eligible task.

→ related card
2. What does update_deadline() do, and when?

When v ≥ vd, the request is used up. It sets vd = v + slice·1024/w and requests a reschedule.

→ related card
3. Why does the kernel compare avg ≥ key·load rather than avg/load ≥ key?

Division truncates and loses precision, while cross-multiplication is exact. The cost is a 128-bit product or overflow checks.

→ related card
4. What does place_entity() multiply lag by, and why (W+w)/W?

It multiplies by (W+w)/W because adding the task pulls V toward its own vruntime and shrinks its apparent lag by W/(W+w). Inflating the lag pre-compensates; the derivation is in the math card.

→ related card
5. What state does a sched_delayed task hold?

Its clamped (≤ 0) vlag and its deadline. It stays on the tree, ineligible, aging in virtual time until it is picked or woken.

→ related card
Why
6. Why must V be the weighted average?

So that Σ lagi = 0 (conservation), and V is the fluid-model reference: each task should have received wi·ΔV.

→ related card
7. Why is the tree keyed on deadline but augmented with min_vruntime?

Deadline order gives the answer among the eligible. min_vruntime lets whole subtrees with no eligible task be pruned, so the pick is O(log n).

→ related card
8. Why is lag stored as virtual lag?

Because lag = w·vlag, and vlag does not depend on weight. A reweight becomes one scaling, and comparisons avoid multiplying by w.

→ related card
9. Why do deadlines use slice/weight?

The same request costs less virtual time at higher weight. Heavier tasks therefore get earlier deadlines and more CPU per unit of V.

→ related card
Counterfactual
10. Remove eligibility: what pathological workload appears?

A flood of tiny-slice tasks always holds the earliest deadline and starves the long-slice tasks. Lag is unbounded.

→ related card
11. Every slice is 0.1 ms: what happens to throughput and fairness?

Context-switch and cache costs rise sharply, throughput drops, and the fairness error shrinks to about one slice.

→ related card
12. PLACE_LAG is off: describe an exploit.

Sleep briefly each slice and wake. The negative lag is discarded, so you re-enter near V and never pay back the overconsumption.

→ related card
13. A task's weight doubles while it is queued: what must be rescaled?

Its vruntime, vlag and deadline must be rescaled (the reweight path), with the same lag clamp. Without the clamp the arithmetic can overflow, as in CVE-2024-35985.

→ related card
Adversarial
14. How can a task game EEVDF through sleeping, and which feature closes that?

Short sleeps reset its negative lag. DELAY_DEQUEUE keeps such a task queued and ineligible, so it burns off its lag instead.

→ related card
15. How can unprivileged code shorten its slice to hurt others, and what bounds the damage?

A short sched_runtime makes the task preempt-prone with early deadlines. Eligibility caps its share at its weight, so the damage is extra context switches, not extra CPU.

→ related card
16. How did CVE-2024-35985 turn a weight change into a kernel NULL dereference?

reweight_eevdf() computed vlag without the clamp. Weight is 20-bit fixed point, so the s64 product overflowed. entity_eligible() then returned false for every task, and pick_eevdf() returned NULL.

→ related card
Connection
17. How does EEVDF relate to WF2Q, and why does the eligibility test match?

Both use the same eligibility gate, start time ≤ virtual time. WF2Q uses it to cap how far ahead of GPS a flow can run, and EEVDF uses it to bound lag.

→ related card
18. How does cpu.weight interact with hierarchical group entities?

Each group has a scheduling entity per CPU, weighted from cpu.weight. It competes in its parent's EEVDF, while its inner tasks run their own EEVDF. h_load carries the effective weight.

→ related card
19. How is SCHED_DEADLINE different from a virtual deadline?

SCHED_DEADLINE uses wall-clock runtime, deadline and period, with admission control and CBS. EEVDF's deadlines are virtual, relative to a fair-share reference, and carry no guarantee.

→ related card
Open
20. Can per-runqueue lag bounds compose into a global fairness bound with a periodic balancer?

Open. The balancer equalises load rather than lag, and it runs on timescales much longer than a slice.

→ related card
21. What would a latency-SLO interface look like, instead of a slice?

Open. Candidates include latency-nice, SLO hints passed through sched_ext maps, and proxy-execution-aware slices.

→ related card
22. How should lag be defined across heterogeneous cores?

Open. Capacity-scaling vruntime, as PELT does for utilisation, is the obvious approach but unsettled.

→ related card
